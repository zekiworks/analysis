Setup

The question is which model, at which reasoning setting, makes better decisions in which positions. Two kinds of data answer it: league games and shared positions. A setup is one model with one set of settings: a model with reasoning off and the same model with reasoning on are two setups.

Leagues

Setups play each other in leagues: each league is a single round robin. League tables compare players inside one league only, and leagues are never merged into one overall score. See Leagues.

  • League 1: decision models, plus two programs: Random (a random legal play) and Safest (the play that the fewest opponent rolls hit).
  • League 2: workhorse chat models, closed and open, each in two settings (reasoning low and high, or off and on).
  • League 3: frontier chat models played through their subscriptions, each with reasoning off and on.
  • Anchor: GNU Backgammon at its weak Beginner level plays in all three leagues as a fixed reference player (see the reference-player checks).

Match rules

  • A match is played in rounds of two games with the same dice: in the second game the players swap seats and the same rolls are repeated, so both face the same luck.
  • Three rounds, or fewer: the first player to 5 points after a full round wins if ahead. A tie gets one extra round, then counts as a draw. A game still running after 300 turns goes to the lower pip count for 1 point.
  • Games score 1 point, 2 for a gammon and 3 for a backgammon. There is no doubling cube.
  • League table: 3 points for a match win, 1 for a draw; ties broken by more games won, then fewer games lost.
  • Dice seeds are fixed per league and published. Each match has its own dice (see the dice-sharing test).
  • The v3 leagues play without a clock (see Time).

How a decision is asked

  • Legal plays only. The program generates every legal play and the model chooses one of them, so a model can never make an illegal move. A roll with only one legal play is played automatically and is not a decision.
  • Shuffled order. The legal plays appear in a fixed random order per position, drawn from a published seed. The order never comes from an engine's ranking.
  • Reading the answer. Decision models give a probability for each option. Open chat models give probabilities for each answer letter, read after their reasoning when it is on. Models without probabilities answer one letter.
  • Long play lists. When a position has more legal plays than a model accepts, the plays are split into equal groups. The model picks a winner in each group, then chooses among the winners. The decision page shows when this happened.
  • Invalid answers. An answer that names no option is not retried. The runner plays a random legal play and marks the decision invalid. That play is never counted as the model's choice: it is shown as invalid and left out of every error rate.
  • Retries. Network and server errors are retried. Retry and wait time is kept apart from request time.
  • Memory. Every decision is a separate request. Models are not told how earlier games went.

Game phases

Each position gets the first phase that matches:

  1. Opening: turns 1 to 6, each side's first three moves.
  2. Bear-off: any of GNU Backgammon's bear-off classes.
  3. Race: GNU Backgammon's race class.
  4. Holding: a contact position after turn 6 where one side has escaped all its back checkers and the other keeps one high anchor (2 or more checkers on the opponent's bar, 5- or 4-point, and no other checkers in the opponent's home board or on its bar point). This follows The Gammon Press's definition of a holding game.
  5. Contact: every other position.

Game-plan tags

For analysis only, each contact position after the opening gets the first tag that matches: blitz (a checker on the bar against a home board with 3 or more made points), back game (2 or more anchors in the opponent's home board), holding (the rule above), low anchor (the only anchor is on the opponent's 3-, 2- or ace-point), priming (4 or more made points in a row), or other.

The prompt

The current version is bg-v2. Every player gets it, and it did not change after the first match. It is the format of the earlier version's league #8. Every decision sends these parts in this order:

  1. Rules and strategy: the rules of backgammon, strategy principles (blots and points, distribution, exposure, blocking, hitting, anchors), the best opening plays from computer rollouts, and the match rules. Decision models get a short version; chat models get a longer one with a glossary, game plans and a worked example. Each setup's page says which it got.
  2. Score and where the match stands.
  3. Position from the mover's side: checkers on every point, bar, borne off, pip counts.
  4. Race: the pip lead and whether the two sides are still in contact.
  5. Dice, then the question: which move makes a point in front of the opponent's checkers, reduces your single pieces, or gets you closer to home.
  6. The legal plays in the shuffled order, each followed by what the position looks like after it, computed by the program: hits, points made, blots left and how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit them, points held, longest prime, pips. Chat models see each play under a letter.

There is no move history: in the earlier version, adding the last 10 moves made no measurable difference. The Every decision page shows the exact text each model was sent.

History

The prompt was built one step at a time in the earlier version's leagues; the next section shows each step and what it changed. bg-v2 keeps that format and changes how the models are asked: the plays are shuffled, an invalid answer is not retried, and every setup gets the same rules and question. The earlier version re-asked up to three times and then played the first legal play, which its order favoured.

Rules and strategy as text, not a data structure

The first leagues sent the position as structured data with a short rules summary. Models opened badly: 28% of first moves matched the known best play. With the rules, strategy principles and the opening table as prose, that rose to 77%. Opening play improved, but the middle game did not: games still ran to the turn limit, because both sides kept hitting and re-entering.

A question that names what a good move does

The question before the choices states three goals in plain words: build a point in front of the opponent's checkers, reduce your blots, bring checkers home. It deliberately leaves hitting out, because hitting on almost every move was the problem. The turn-limit share fell from 81% to 71% (this league also switched to the self-hosted Decider v1.1); hitting barely changed.

Compute the consequences in code, not in the model

Backgammon decisions turn on numbers a language model cannot work out reliably from a list of notations: which checkers are left alone, how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit them, which points are made. Earlier, each option was just its notation, like bar/19 13/11*, and the model had to imagine the resulting position. Now the program computes those numbers for every option and the model compares them. This is the change that ended the endless games: hits fell from about 60% to under 10% of moves and the average game from about 270 turns to about 50.

Opening play got worse at the same time, from about 80% to between 58% and 69% best plays. The metrics after each option compete with the opening table earlier in the prompt, and on the first move several plays look similar on these numbers.

Recent moves, for longer plans

Each decision is a separate request, so a model cannot see how the position came about. League #13 adds the last 10 moves of the game, both sides. Ten turns add about 200 tokens. More would eat into Open-Jev's 4,096-token limit, which it applies to the whole question repeated for every option.

It made no measurable difference. League #13's error rate was 39.1 (± 2.2), against 38.6 (± 1.2) for #8, #11 and #12 together, and the finishing order barely changed. Ten moves may be too few, or these models may not use them.

Error rate

Wins and losses depend heavily on the dice. A backgammon engine can instead judge every decision directly. GNU Backgammon 1.08 evaluates every legal play at 2-ply, in cubeless money equity (expected points per game, match score ignored). The loss of a decision is the equity of the best play minus the equity of the play made. A play that ends the game scores its points exactly.

Error rate
500 × mean 2-ply equity loss per decision. Lower is better. This is the checker-play performance rating (PR) that backgammon players use.
Decision
A roll with 2 or more legal plays. Forced moves count in no rate.
Invalid answers
Left out of the error rate and shown separately, with their count and share.
Engine's best play
Share of decisions where the play made lost less than 0.001 equity.
Blunder rate
Share of decisions where the play made lost more than 0.08 equity.
Tail error
The 0.95 quantile of the loss per decision, and the mean loss of the worst 0.05 of decisions.
Near-tie
The two best plays are within 0.02 equity. Close plays are not rolled out; the gap groups on the Phases page show how much of a setup's error comes from them.

Is the risk worth it? Definitions

This is the main research question: can a model tell when a risk is worth taking?

Hitting rolls
How many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit a blot after a play, as the model was shown it next to that play. The program counts them by generating the opponent's legal plays for each roll.
Safest play
The legal play that leaves the fewest hitting rolls. Several plays can tie.
Safety pays
A safest play is the engine's best play, or loses less than 0.001 equity.
Risk pays
Otherwise: the engine's best play leaves more hitting rolls than the safest play.
Took the risk
The play made leaves more hitting rolls than the safest play.
Played safe
The play made is one of the safest plays.
Left out
Decisions where every legal play leaves the same number of hitting rolls. They are not risk decisions.

Two headline numbers per setup: in Risk pays decisions, how often it takes the risk; in Safety pays decisions, how often it takes a needless risk.

Limit: this measures one kind of risk, being hit. Gammon risk and priming are not in it.

Statistics and verdicts

  • Intervals are 0.95 bootstrap intervals with 2000 resamples and a fixed seed (20261021).
  • League decisions resample whole games, because moves within a game are not independent.
  • Shared positions resample positions.
  • Comparisons of two setups on the same positions are always paired: the interval is of the per-position difference in loss.
  • Minimum sample: a cell with fewer than 200 decisions shows "Too few decisions" instead of a value.
  • League placings are descriptive. Middle places move between leagues with the dice.

Verdicts

For reasoning (low against high, off against on) and any comparison of A against B:

VerdictRule
High helpsThe paired 95% interval of the error difference (high minus low, or B minus A) lies entirely below zero.
High hurtsThe interval lies entirely above zero.
No clear benefitThe interval includes zero. This does not mean the two are the same.
Depends on the positionPhase-level effects point in different directions, each with an interval that excludes zero.

Every phase-level verdict is exploratory. "Worse moves" and "more time or cost with no shown gain" are kept as two separate findings and never merged into "high is bad".

Versions and play order

Numbers from different benchmark versions or prompt formats are never put in one ranking or chart. Every number carries its version.

Earlier version Plays were listed in the runner's order, which tends to put the best play early. Re-asked with the same plays in a random order on 1,000 positions, Gemini 4 Argon's error rate changed by +1.9 (95% interval −0.2 to 4.2), so a help from the order is possible but not proven. The current version shuffles the plays for every setup.

The earlier version also scored differently: every legal play at 0-ply, then the five best and the chosen play again at 2-ply. The current version evaluates every legal play at 2-ply.

Confidence

  • Sources. A setup's confidence is its probability for the play it chose. It comes from one of three sources: decision-model probability, letter probability, or none.
  • N/A for choice-only setups. Confidence metrics (AUROC, ECE, Brier score, risk–coverage) are N/A for setups that return only a choice. A recorded 1.0 from those setups is not 100% confidence.
  • Same source only. Confidence is compared only between setups with the same confidence source.
  • Group splits. Decisions whose plays were split into groups are left out of the confidence analyses: the probability then belongs to a smaller choice.
  • Confident and badly wrong: the setup gave its play at least 0.9 and the play lost more than 0.08 equity.

Time

  • Request time runs from sending a request to receiving the answer. Time spent on retries and on waiting after provider errors is kept separate and is not part of it.
  • Several requests can be in flight at once, so on our own hardware the time depends on the load.
  • Time per decision is shown as the median and the 90th percentile.
  • Time budget: the share of decisions slower than 30 seconds.
  • The v3 leagues had no clock, so no game could be lost on time. The risk of going over a time budget in play was therefore not measured; the share above only shows how often a setup needed more time.

Limits

  • One kind of risk. "Is the risk worth it?" measures one kind of risk, being hit. Gammon risk and priming are not in it.
  • Checker plays only. The games have no doubling cube, so the error rate leaves out cube decisions. The human scale is a rough guide.
  • The engine evaluation is not the strongest available. Plays are judged at 2-ply with GNU Backgammon, not by rollouts, in cubeless money equity with match score ignored. Small differences between close plays can be wrong. GNU Backgammon learned standard play, so its scores for early Nackgammon positions are likely less accurate.
  • The prompt steers. The strategy text and the question say what a good move looks like. The results measure how well models follow that guidance with the numbers in front of them, as much as how well they play.
  • Strategy text is in training data. Rules, opening tables and strategy articles are online. Backgammon is not an unfamiliar task.
  • Setups get different inputs. Rules text length and option groups differ between setups; rankings mark rows that got different inputs. The shared positions avoid group splits.
  • Providers vary. A hosted model can change without notice, and providers differ in quantization. We log them; we cannot control them.
  • Models only play each other and the anchor. Their strength against human players is not measured.
  • No learning from experience. Every decision is a separate request; models are not told how earlier games went.

Method experiments

Two experiments with GNU Backgammon players only, run to choose how the leagues work. They are method findings, not results about any model.

Sharing dice across matches

Method finding, not a model result

Each match has its own dice. We tested whether giving round r of every match the same roll schedule would make a league table less a matter of luck. The test used 8 GNU Backgammon players of known strength and no model, and replayed a round robin 500 times under each scheme. The players' order was checked in a separate long run of 11,200 matches: in all 28 pairs the stronger player scored more league points per match.

  • Shared schedules made the table noisier. League points scattered more: standard deviation ×1.10 (95% interval 1.06 to 1.14); variance ×1.20 (1.11 to 1.29).
  • The order got slightly worse. Rank correlation with the true order fell from 0.890 to 0.864 (difference −0.026, 95% interval −0.038 to −0.014). The stronger of two neighbours finished above the weaker in about 72% of replays under both schemes.
  • Why. Mirrored games already cancel most of the dice luck within a match. Sharing schedules across matches cancels nothing more, and it puts all of a player's matches on the same dice, so their luck adds up: two matches of the same player correlated +0.042 with shared schedules against +0.001 without.

So the leagues keep separate dice per match. The test players are not language models, whose play varies more from match to match.

Checks of the reference player

Method finding, not a model result

The anchor is GNU Backgammon 1.08 at its Beginner level (0-ply, deterministic noise). We checked whether it is the same player on every machine.

  • It is reproducible on x86-64. Replaying all 4,387 recorded anchor decisions on the x86-64 build gives the recorded play in 4,387; on an ARM build of the same package, in 31.0%.
  • Another build plays differently. On the 1,000 v1 positions, the x86-64 and ARM builds choose different plays in 659. With the noise off, they differ in 6, all exact ties, so the engine's own scoring does not depend on the build. Error rate 21.8 on x86-64 against 16.8 on ARM.
  • Why. The noise code sums hash bytes as char, which is signed on x86-64, but subtracts the mean of unsigned bytes. On x86-64 every noise draw is shifted by about −6.9 standard deviations: the anchor understates its own winning chances and plays for gammons.
  • Gammons in self-play (300 games each): 71.7% of games end in a gammon or backgammon on x86-64, 40.3% on ARM, 30.0% without noise.

So the v3 anchor is "GNU Backgammon 1.08 Beginner, x86-64 build". It is a fixed reference, not GNU Backgammon's Beginner as published elsewhere. See also About the anchor.
