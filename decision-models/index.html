When should you trust an AI model to make a decision?

When its confident answers turn out to be right.

We tested 17 decision models to find out which ones know when they are right, and how much work they can take off a large language model (LLM).

Most AI tests are in English. We tested Turkish exam questions (native, not translated).

What we did

  1. We asked 17 decision models 2,198 exam questions. A decision model does not write text. It picks one of the options we give it and gives a confidence score from 0% to 100%.
  2. We asked two questions. Which models got the answers right? And when a model was confident, was it right? The confidence score tells us how confident a model is. Our tests tell us whether we can trust that score.
  3. We put 14 of them in front of three LLMs: Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. For each pair we set a confidence cutoff: the lowest confidence at which the decision model's kept answers were right at least as often as the LLM's answers to the same questions. At or above the cutoff, we kept the decision model's answer; below it, the LLM answered. We set the cutoff on half of the questions and checked it on the other half, and repeated this with 300 random splits of recorded answers.

Our pick is Perplexity's Decider v1.1. It took the most questions off each LLM. Fastino's GLiDE came second each time, but its own calls cost 21 times as much as Decider's.

When Decider answered a question, the LLM did not have to answer it. We pay for Decider on every question. We pay for the LLM only on the questions passed to it. So the LLM cost less to run: 48% less for Sonnet, 25% less for Opus and 21% less for Astra, at each company's list prices; we replayed recorded answers. Where decision models fall short

Decision models are weak at grammar questions. Decider got 89.0% of the reading questions right, but only 57.5% of the grammar questions. On their own, the best decision models get about two answers in three right; the best LLMs get 98%. That is why we keep a decision model's answer only when it is confident.

The key idea

What we mean by “confidence”

Confidence is the score a model gives the answer it picks, from 0% to 100%.

A decision model splits 100% across the options. Here is one example question:

Scores a decision model gives the five options of one question (an example)
OptionABCDE
Score1%1%96%1%1%

Decider picks C, because C has the biggest score. Its confidence is 96%.

Confidence is the model's own score, not a promise. A model can say 99% and still be wrong. So we check every answer we keep against the answer key.

We call the lowest confidence we accept the cutoff. A higher cutoff keeps fewer answers and lets fewer mistakes through. The most confident quarter is the cutoff that keeps a quarter of Decider's answers; on this test it falls at 96.05%.

Suppose Decider picks C with 96% confidence. With a 99% cutoff, the LLM answers that question. If Decider had given 99.5%, we would keep its answer.

Chat models such as Claude, GPT and Gemini work differently. They write an answer, and we ask them to write a confidence too. That number is what the model says; we do not read it from inside the model. Some models say useful numbers, and GPT-6 Astra did. Others do not: GPT-6 Luna said 99% or more on 830 answers, and 137 of them were wrong. When we read the same model the decision-model way, through OpenAI's Decisions API, it got 3 of 368 wrong. The two Luna setups also sent questions differently (1 against 16 per request), so this example does not prove that the method alone made the difference.

How it works

How Decider and Opus work together

  1. We ask Decider to pick an answer and give its confidence.
  2. We compare that confidence with the cutoff.
  3. If it reaches the cutoff, we keep Decider's answer. Opus never sees that question.
  4. If it does not, Opus answers, and we use Opus's answer.

The LLM does not check the answers we keep. It answers only the remaining questions.

The same steps work with any stronger model; we also tested Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. We choose the cutoff on questions with known answers, then test it on different questions. A cutoff that works for one model or one task may not work for another.

Why Decider

Why Decider v1.1?

Decider v1.1 is not the smartest model. On its own it gets 67% of these questions right; the best chat models get 98%. But it is fast, it is cheap, and it knows when it is right. That is what the first step needs.

  • Fast. It answers one question in a typical 0.23 seconds through Perplexity's API. Fastino GLiDE takes 2.09 seconds. Claude Opus 5.5 takes a typical 13.40 seconds per request of 16 questions.
  • Cheap. It costs $0.008 per 1,000 questions. Opus costs $4.93 for the same 1,000 questions.
  • The most confident-and-right answers. At a 99% cutoff it kept 349 answers, with no mistakes. The other decision models with no mistakes at that cutoff kept 121 (GLiDE) and 21 (Decider v1).
  • It tells its good answers from its bad ones. Its ranking score (AUROC) is 0.84, the highest of all decision models; Claude Opus 5.5's written confidence scores 0.84. The ranking score asks: if we take one right answer and one wrong answer at random, how often does the right one have the higher confidence? 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect.
  • Its numbers mean what they say. On average, its confidence and its real hit rate differ by 3.8 percentage points; we call this the calibration gap (ECE). Opus's written numbers differ by 16.5 points.
  • It does not change its mind. We asked all 2,198 questions five times. It gave the same answer every time.
  • A big step up from version 1. It is 6.5 points more accurate (66.9% against 60.4%) and keeps 17 times more answers at 99% (349 against 21). It also costs half as much, because Perplexity cut its price between the two runs.

GLiDE comes close. In front of Opus, with the cutoff chosen on half the questions, it takes less of the work than Decider (21.8% against 25.2%) but loses less accuracy (0.10 against 0.23 points). Decider wins on speed and price: 0.23 against 2.09 seconds, and $0.008 against $0.16 per 1,000 questions.

Confidence

What happens if we keep only answers with at least 99% confidence?

This is a separate check with one fixed cutoff. We keep every recorded answer with a confidence of at least 99%. Every other question needs another answer. We do not add a second model's answers in this chart.

We kept 349 of Decider v1.1's answers, 15.9% of the questions. All 349 were right by the checked answer key. The other 1,849 questions need another answer, from another model or a person.

Decider v1.1 kept more answers with no mistakes than any other decision model at this cutoff. GLiDE kept 121, also with no mistakes. Luna through the Decisions API kept 368, with three mistakes. Jev kept 312, with one mistake. Liquid d1 kept 274, with one mistake.

No mistakes in a sample does not prove zero risk. For Decider v1.1, the 95% interval for the error rate runs from 0% to 1.1%. For GLiDE, it runs from 0% to 3.1%. GLiDE kept fewer answers, so its interval is wider. A 95% interval is not a model's confidence: Decider v1.1's 99% is its own score for one answer, while the 95% interval is the range our statistics give for the error rate we measured. These intervals describe sampling uncertainty under the method's assumptions. They do not promise an error limit on your next task.

Accuracy

How often do decision models get the answer right?

On all 2,198 questions, GLiDE answered 67.9% correctly and Decider v1.1 66.9%. GLiDE's score was one percentage point higher. Our test did not establish a difference between them.

The stronger chat models answered many more questions right: Claude Opus 5.5 (low reasoning) reached 90.4%, Claude Sonnet 5.5 (high reasoning) 85.0% and GPT-6 Astra (low reasoning) 97.6%. We put Decider v1.1 first to keep its most confident answers at a low cost, and a stronger model such as Claude Opus 5.5 answers the rest.

Reading and grammar give different results. Decider v1.1 scored 89.0% on reading and 57.5% on grammar. Gemma 4 31B scored 90.9% and 65.6%. So Gemma 4 31B beat every decision model on both, even though its grammar score was much lower than its own reading score. GPT-6 Astra scored 97.0% and 97.9%.

The test has 656 reading questions and 1,542 grammar questions. Grammar makes up about 70% of all questions, so it counts for more in the overall score. Look at both task scores before you choose a model.

Training, model size and task difficulty could all affect these results. This test does not separate their effects.

How the models answer

A decision model scores the options we give it. A chat model writes an answer and may reason first. We count GPT-6 Luna through OpenAI's Decisions API as a decision model, because that service returns a probability for each option.

Decider v1.1 cost an estimated $0.008 per 1,000 questions. Its typical response time was 233 milliseconds per request in our setup. A millisecond is one thousandth of a second.

GPT-6 Astra (low reasoning) used about 21 output tokens per question, including reported reasoning tokens. A token is a small piece of text that a model reads or writes. Output length alone does not tell us how much computation a model used or why its accuracy was higher.

More reasoning helped some models. Claude Sonnet 5.5's grammar score rose from 52.2% to 85.0% when we changed its reasoning setting from low to high. Its estimated cost almost doubled.

How to read the comparison groups

Two setups share a letter when our test did not detect an accuracy difference between them. A shared letter does not mean their scores are equal. The letters compare all 630 pairs of setups and adjust for the number of comparisons.

How we calculate the letters

For each pair of setups, we use an exact McNemar test. It compares the two setups on the questions only one of them got right. The Holm correction then adjusts each p value for the number of pairs we test. Two setups share a letter when this test does not tell them apart. Comparing scores explains more.

Repeated answers

Does the model give the same answer again?

We asked the same questions five times with the same settings. Decider v1.1 chose the same answer every time on all 2,198 questions. It also gave the same wrong answer every time on 728 questions: every question it got wrong at least once.

Stable answers make a system easier to inspect. They do not make a wrong answer right.

Live test

What happened in a live test?

We ran the two-step setup live, with Decider v1.1 in front of each of the three LLMs. Decider v1.1's answers came from its recorded run; each LLM answered the questions it passed on, with new calls. We split the questions in half at random, keeping the questions about one passage together, and tested on one half: 1,099 questions. Each LLM also answered all 1,099 of them alone, in a separate run; for Sonnet and Astra, that run comes from our earlier live tests on the same questions.

We tested all three rules. With the cutoff chosen on half the questions, we set the cutoff on the other half. With the most confident quarter, Decider v1.1 kept its 275 most confident answers among the test questions. With the fixed 99% rule, it kept every answer at 99% or more, 176 of them.

With the cutoff chosen on half the questions, Decider v1.1 kept 51%, 20% and 20% of the questions in front of Sonnet, Opus and Astra, and the estimated cost fell 37%, 19% and 12%. The replay of recorded answers gave 48%, 25% and 21%. The replay averages 300 splits of the questions and gives every question passed to the LLM the same average cost. The live test used one split, and each LLM's own cost for the questions passed to it: a question passed to Sonnet cost 27% more than its average question, one passed to Opus 1% more and one passed to Astra 10% more.

With the most confident quarter, the estimated cost fell 18%, 23% and 18% for Sonnet, Opus and Astra. With the fixed 99% rule, it fell 11%, 18% and 15%.

Before the tests, we chose the largest accuracy loss we would accept: 0.5 percentage points. In six of the nine tests, the observed change stayed within it; in the tests with Sonnet and the cutoff chosen on half the questions, with Opus and the most confident quarter and with Opus and the fixed 99% rule, the observed loss was larger. In eight of the nine tests, the 95% interval also allows a larger loss, so those tests do not establish that the loss stays within our limit. None of the nine tests established a change in accuracy from the LLM alone: the exact McNemar test gave p = 0.25 or more.

Our first live tests used the earlier Decider v1, with the cutoff chosen on half the questions: in front of Sonnet, it kept 31.2% of the questions and the estimated cost fell 21.9%, with accuracy 0.09 points lower; in front of Astra, it kept 17.7% of the questions and the estimated cost fell 16.9%, with accuracy 0.36 points lower.

We also ran two cheaper models alone on the same 1,099 questions. GPT-6.1 Sol (low reasoning) scored 96.82% at an estimated $1.20 per 1,000 questions. Gemini 3.8 Flash (low reasoning) scored 91.08% at an estimated $0.27 per 1,000 questions. They can be good choices when their accuracy is enough for the task.

How to try this on your own task

  1. Test the answers the first model would keep. Check them against known answers and count the mistakes.
  2. Choose the cutoff on one set of questions, then check it on different questions.
  3. Measure the accuracy of the whole two-step setup, not only of the kept answers.
  4. Compare its total cost and response time with the LLM alone.

We tested 29 models in 36 setups on 2,198 Turkish exam questions about reading and grammar. A setup is one model with one set of settings. We measured four things: how often each model was right, whether its confidence helped us find answers we could keep, whether it gave the same answers when we asked again, and what its requests cost and how long they took.

What we found

Decider v1.1 is fast and cheap. Perplexity's Decider v1.1 is a decision model: it picks one of the answer options we give it and gives each option a score. It writes no text. It answered one question in a typical 0.23 seconds (the median: half the requests took less time), for $0.008 per 1,000 questions.

We put Decider in front of a stronger chat model, a model that writes its answer as text. We kept Decider's answer when its confidence was high, and the chat model answered the rest. Confidence is the score a model gives the answer it picked, from 0% to 100%. We call this a two-step setup. The savings below come from a replay: we combined answers we had already recorded and made no new calls. We give accuracy changes in percentage points, the difference between two percentages; after this, we write “points”.

In front of Claude Sonnet 5.5 at high reasoning, Decider's most confident quarter (its 25% most confident answers) cut the bill by 25%, and accuracy was 0.59 points higher (replayed recorded answers). With the cutoff chosen on half the questions, Decider took 48% of the work and cut the bill by 48%, with accuracy 0.10 points lower (replayed recorded answers). A cutoff is the lowest confidence at which we keep an answer.

In front of Claude Opus 5.5, the most confident quarter took a quarter of the work. Decider got 546 of its 550 answers right. Opus's bill fell 25%, and accuracy was 0.09 points lower (replayed recorded answers).

In front of GPT-6 Astra, the bill fell 21% with the cutoff chosen on half the questions and 25% with the most confident quarter. Accuracy was 0.13 points lower and 0.14 points lower (replayed recorded answers).

At a cutoff of 99%, Decider kept 349 answers with 0 wrong, and each of the three bills fell about 16% (replayed recorded answers). Fastino's GLiDE kept 121 answers at 99%, with 0 wrong. Fewer answers leave more doubt about the true error rate.

GLiDE had the highest accuracy among the decision models: 67.9%, against 66.9% for Decider v1.1. Our test did not establish a difference between them. GLiDE cost about 21 times as much as Decider for the first model's calls. That ratio does not apply to the cost of the whole two-step setup.

Gemma 4 31B, a chat model, scored higher than every decision model on reading (90.9%) and on grammar (65.6%). Its confidence matched its real hit rate less closely than the best decision models' confidence did. Accuracy, ranking answers by confidence and confidence that matches the hit rate are separate strengths.

Several models did better with more reasoning, that is, when the chat model worked on the problem before it answered. Sonnet's grammar score rose from 52.2% to 85.0%. Gemini 3.8 Flash's rose from 92.5% to 98.5%. DeepSeek also improved, but that comparison changed both reasoning and random sampling, so it does not separate the two.

How a model gives its confidence matters. GPT-6 Luna through OpenAI's Decisions API ranked its answers better than Luna writing its own confidence. Its ranking score (AUROC), how often a right answer gets higher confidence than a wrong one, was 0.82 against 0.69. But it got fewer answers right: 63.6% against 68.3%. The access method and the number of questions per request also differed.

Stable answers can repeat mistakes. Decider v1.1 changed 0 answers across five full runs, and it gave the same wrong answer every time on 728 questions. Open Jev also changed 0 answers across its full runs.

Cheaper chat models remain alternatives. On all 2,198 questions, GPT-6.1 Sol scored 96.7% at an estimated cost 79% below Astra's; Astra scored 97.6%. Gemini 3.8 Flash at high reasoning scored 98.1%; the test over all pairs did not establish a difference from Astra. Its cost estimate uses the introductory price recorded for that run. Decider also works in front of Sol: at 99% it took 15% off Sol's bill, with no change in accuracy (replayed recorded answers).

Related models can behave differently. Decider v1, Decider v1.1, Clef and Open Jev share one Qwen base model. Decider v1.1 scored 6.5 points above v1. Perplexity reports changes to both the training data and how the model reads the input, so this test does not tell us which change made the difference.

Among the decision models, Luna through the Decisions API had the smallest calibration gap (ECE), the average distance between confidence and the real hit rate: 2.4 points. Decider v1.1 had the highest ranking score: 0.84. Neither model led on every measure.

Erk-14B is Qwen3-14B with extra Turkish training by eCloud. Its confidence matched its hit rate more closely than its Qwen base did: a calibration gap of 13.5 points against 41.0. We did not detect an accuracy difference (45.0% against 45.7%). The extra training may have changed more than language knowledge.

What questions did we ask?

The questions come from one practice book for the Turkish section of the TYT, Turkey's university entrance exam. There are 656 reading questions and 1,542 grammar questions across 20 topics.

Reading questions ask about the meaning of words, sentences and passages. Grammar questions ask about things such as word endings, word types and sentence structure. The test has more grammar questions, so we report the two kinds separately.

We check four things: whether the answer is right, whether confidence helps us spot mistakes, whether the answer stays the same when we ask again, and what answering costs in money and time.

Other benchmarks ask related questions. TurkishMMLU3 covers more than 10,000 Turkish questions across nine school subjects. HELM4 compares accuracy with measures such as calibration and efficiency. Our study looks at the decision models' own option scores, and at using those scores to decide when another model should answer.

We expected more mistakes, or less useful confidence, on tasks that models saw less of in training. We do not know what most models saw in training, so these results cannot show that this is the cause. Even the Qwen and Erk comparison may reflect changes beyond language training.

We measure results on these tasks. We do not compute a separate score for understanding.

How we chose which answers to keep

We tried a simple process. A cheap model answers first. We keep its answer when its confidence reaches a cutoff, and a stronger model answers the rest. This is often called routing. Earlier work such as FrugalGPT5 and RouteLLM6 also studies ways to give questions to different models to cut cost.

The first model must be cheap enough that asking it does not use up the saving. We count the cost of asking it every question, not only the questions whose answers we keep. Decider v1.1 cost $0.008 per 1,000 questions; Claude Opus 5.5 alone cost $4.93.

For each pair of models, we split the questions at random into two halves. Questions that share a reading passage stayed in the same half. We chose the cutoff on the first half. Then we kept it fixed and tested it on the other half, which we held out for testing. We repeated this 300 times. We call this rule the cutoff chosen on half the questions. Both models' answers come from recorded runs, so every result in this section replays recorded answers.

The question share is the average share of the test halves that the first model answered. The accuracy change compares the combined answers with the second model's answers on the same test questions. “Test splits with lower combined accuracy” shows how often the combined result fell below the second model alone.

Does the first model know which questions another model will get wrong?

We also asked whether a decision model's uncertainty points to the questions that Opus, Sonnet or Astra gets wrong.

Here we measure uncertainty from all the option scores, not only the score of the chosen answer. A model has low uncertainty when it puts most of its score on one option. It has high uncertainty when it spreads its score across several options. The mathematical name for this measure is entropy.

The link was weak. The most uncertain 10% of questions held 12% to 18% of the questions on which at least one of the stronger models made a mistake. A random 10% of questions would hold about 10% of them on average.

Uncertainty may help direct a little extra attention, but it does not reliably predict a stronger model's mistakes. Low confidence does not show that Astra will fail. The table gives the ranking measures and the tests behind this conclusion.

    Does higher confidence mean a more reliable answer?

    We ask two separate questions about confidence, for all 36 setups. We also show where each score comes from: an option score, an answer letter probability, a written estimate or agreement across ten answers.

    First: does the model usually give a right answer a higher score than a wrong one? If so, the score can help us choose which answers to keep. We call this confidence ranking.

    We measure it with the ranking score (AUROC). Pick one right answer and one wrong answer at random. The ranking score is how often the right answer has the higher confidence, with half credit when the two scores are equal. A ranking score of 0.50 is chance; 1.00 means every right answer ranks above every wrong one. It is not the model's accuracy.

    Keeping only the most confident answers and leaving the rest is known as selective prediction.10 The “most confident 20%” column takes that share of answers with the highest scores and checks how many are right. It does not mean answers with a confidence above 20%. The 50% and 80% columns work the same way. If the cut falls among answers with the same score, we average over those answers.

    These percentages describe only the answers we keep. The accuracy of a two-step setup also includes the second model's answers to the other questions.

    Second: do the confidence percentages match how often the model is right? If a model gives 100 answers a score near 80%, about 80 of them should be right. We call this match calibration.

    We measure the mismatch with the calibration gap (expected calibration error, ECE).11 We sort the answers into ten confidence groups: 0% to below 10%, 10% to below 20%, and so on. In each group, we compare the average confidence with the share of right answers. Then we average those gaps, giving larger groups more weight, and show the result in points. A smaller gap means a closer match. A gap of zero means a match within these groups; it does not prove that every single score is right.

    The table shows the number of answers in each group. The chart leaves out groups with fewer than 20 answers, because they give very little evidence. The table keeps all groups.

    A model can rank its answers well while its percentages are too high or too low. Open Jev shows this: it got 144 of the 144 answers it scored from 50% to below 60% right. On these questions, those scores were far too low.

    What did a score of 99% or more mean in practice?

    The answer depends on where the score comes from.

    At a cutoff of 99%, Jev got 311 of 312 answers right, d1 273 of 274, Decider v1 21 of 21, Decider v1.1 349 of 349 and GLiDE 121 of 121. GPT-6 Luna through the Decisions API got 365 of 368 right. Luna writing its own confidence got 693 of 830 right.

    The number of answers matters. For Decider v1's 21 answers, the 95% interval for the error rate is 0% to 15.5%. For Decider v1.1's 349, it is 0% to 1.1%. For GLiDE's 121, it is 0% to 3.1%. None of these promises the same rate on future questions.

    For Gemma, the score is the probability of writing the chosen answer letter. At scores of 99% or more, Gemma got 1,450 of 1,782 answers right, about 81%. This does not mean that Gemma told us it was 99% confident.

    DeepSeek with reasoning gave every answer letter a score of at least 99%, wrong answers included. It got about 92% of them right. In that setup, this very high letter probability did little to find mistakes.

    The main figures use the answer key after our review. The optional columns also show the results before the review, so you can see the effect of removing doubtful questions (see the limits of the answer key).

    Can a different confidence method make the same answers easier to judge?

    To answer this, we kept one set of Qwen 3.8 27B answers fixed, 1,191 right out of 2,198, and scored those same answers in four ways. This separates the quality of the answers from the quality of the score we use to judge them.

    Qwen's written confidence barely ranked right answers above wrong ones: its ranking score was about 0.51. The Qwen option score, from its probability for each answer, reached about 0.78, and agreement across ten answers about 0.78. Open Jev's scores reached about 0.82.

    The three Qwen methods use the same model. Open Jev is a separately trained model based on Qwen, so its row changes the model that gives the score as well as the method. Earlier studies found that written confidence tends to bunch at high values12, while the answer probabilities of large models on multiple-choice questions can match their hit rates well13.

    Asking Qwen ten times also lets us pick the most common answer. That approach scored 54.3%, against 54.2% for the run that wrote a confidence score. Even when all ten answers agreed, only about 85% were right. Agreement can help, but several answers can repeat the same mistake.

    This comparison shows why we test the confidence method instead of trusting an answer that sounds confident.

    What can these results tell us about understanding?

    A model may answer a passage question correctly and still miss a grammar rule in the same language. That tells us its performance changes from task to task. It does not, by itself, settle whether the model understands language.

    The settings also matter. Sonnet answered 52.2% of the grammar questions correctly with low reasoning and 85.0% with high reasoning. DeepSeek went from 56.6% to 91.4% on grammar when we changed both its reasoning and its sampling. So we describe what a model can do under stated conditions before we make a broad claim about its understanding.

    These tests measure abilities the models already have. They do not measure how quickly a model learned them. Mitchell and Krakauer discuss different meanings of understanding.1 Chollet separates a skill a model has from its ability to learn new skills.2 Our results concern the first.

    Why might reading and grammar differ?

    One possibility: reading draws on patterns that carry over between languages, while school grammar depends more on local rules and conventions. Differences in training, question difficulty and question format could also explain the gap. We did not control these factors well enough to choose between the explanations.

    Confidence can also change by task. The ranking score (AUROC) measures how often a right answer gets higher confidence than a wrong one. Jev's ranking score fell from 0.89 on reading to 0.72 on grammar. Open Jev's fell from 0.89 to 0.75. A score that helps on reading may help less on grammar.

    An Arize evaluation reports a stronger ranking score for Jev on English tasks with two labels. Our test differs in language, task and number of options. So one result cannot stand in for the other.

    The table gives each setup's ranking score on reading and on grammar, with its 95% interval, and the reading score minus the grammar score. A ranking score of 0.50 is chance; 1.00 is perfect. Where both intervals reach 0.50, the evidence does not show better than chance ranking on either kind of question. We still show the observed scores.

    What did adapting Qwen change?

    Open Jev adds trained parts to a Qwen model: a small adapter, a part that scores the options, and a setting that adjusts the probability scale. Open Jev scored 3.8 percentage points above Qwen's plain yes or no scoring. That gap passed the test adjusted for the 41 selected comparisons (p = 0.0066). The ranking score was 0.80 for Qwen's yes or no scoring and 0.82 for Open Jev.

    Open Jev's probability scale still underestimated its success on our questions. Its highest confidence on any question was 64.98%. Yet all 144 answers with confidence from 50% to below 60% were right.

    In a separate analysis on version 1 of the question set, we adjusted the probability scale. The calibration gap (ECE: the average distance between confidence and the real hit rate) fell from 22.8 to 3.0 percentage points. The chosen answers did not change, so accuracy did not either. The ranking score moved from 0.8193 to 0.8191. The main results use the released model without this adjustment. Accuracy, ranking and calibration move separately, so each needs its own measurement.

    What would stronger evidence look like?

    We could change the words of a question and keep the relationship the answer depends on. We could then change that relationship and check that the answer changes too. McCoy, Pavlick and Linzen show how such controlled examples can reveal shortcuts that ordinary accuracy scores miss.14

    We could also teach an unfamiliar rule with a fixed number of examples and test new combinations. That would test learning and generalisation more directly.

    Some research looks inside models for the structures behind particular abilities.15 Our study does not look at those structures. Other research separates generalisation across wording from generalisation across reasoning problems.16 Both point to more targeted experiments than one overall score can give.

    What changed when we repeated the same questions?

    We repeated 8 of the 21 setups that take one question per request. Each ran five times on all 2,198 questions. We also repeated 6 of the 15 setups that take 16 questions per request. Each of these also ran five times, on a fixed set of 200 questions, with the same questions grouped in each request.

    On all questions, Decider v1.1 changed no answers and Open Jev no answers. Decider v1 changed two answers. Jev changed about 14% of its answers, d1 about 10%, Celeris 1 about 9% and Qwen's yes or no scoring about 7%. Qwen with written confidence changed 44 answers, about 2%.

    On the 200 question set, Astra changed its answer on 1 question, Opus on 20 questions and Sonnet with high reasoning on 31 questions. Compare these counts only within this group. The two groups use different questions and different request sizes.

    We also count the questions where a model gave the same wrong answer in every run. Decider v1.1 did this on 728 of the 728 questions it got wrong at least once. A model can give the same answer every time and still be wrong, so a repeated answer is not enough on its own.

    Gemma's stored repeat runs used one server session and changed its answer on 0 questions. A later rerun after a server restart changed some answers, but we did not save it. So the table cannot measure that restart effect. We stored the Gemma and DeepSeek repeats before we started to collect answer letter probabilities, so those repeats have no confidence scores.

    How do we tell a score gap from ordinary variation?

    Both models answer the same questions. So we look at the questions only one of them got right. If model A is right and model B wrong many more times than the reverse, that is evidence of a difference. We use an exact McNemar test for this: a paired test that uses only the questions where one model was right and the other wrong.17 Testing many pairs gives more chances to flag a difference by accident. So we adjust the p values with the Holm method, which reduces those false alarms.

    The comparison table below adjusts across 41 selected pairs. The comparison groups in the accuracy table (setups that share a letter) adjust across all 630 pairs. These are different groups of tests, so a pair can pass one adjustment and not the other.

    Astra scored 0.9 percentage points above Sol. On the 36 questions where only one of them was right, Astra was right on 28 and Sol on 8. The adjusted p value was 0.032 in the selected comparison table and 0.088 across all pairs. The first is below 0.05; the second is not.

    A p value says how unusual a split at least this uneven would be if the two models had equal accuracy. It is not the chance that the models are equal. It is not the chance that the result is wrong.

    DeepSeek with reasoning scored 1.6 points above Opus with low reasoning, but the adjusted p value was 0.53. We did not establish a difference. That does not show that their accuracy matches, either. Many comparisons in language research have too few questions to detect gaps of this size: their statistical power is low.18

    A small gap may need more questions to measure well. So we report both the observed gap and its uncertainty.

    How Open Jev turns option scores into probabilities

    Open Jev starts with a Qwen model. It adds a small trained adapter and a scoring part. The adapter holds 15.5 million adjustable values, about 0.06% of the base model's size. It changes how the base model behaves without replacing all its weights (the stored values that shape a model). The technical name is a LoRA adapter.

    For each option, Open Jev builds a prompt with the question and that proposed answer. The question part shows all the options, but the model scores each of these prompts on its own.

    The scoring part (the scoring head) gives each option a number. A formula called softmax turns these numbers into probabilities that add up to 100%. Before that step, the released setup divides the scores by 2.53. This setting, the calibration temperature, makes the probabilities more or less concentrated. It is a setting, not a confidence percentage.

    The scoring part starts from Qwen's preference for "Yes" over "No". Training adjusts the adapter and the scoring part from that starting point. The model card explains the training stages and the data its makers used to choose the temperature.

    Jev and Open Jev also offer a rescaled score. It puts equal probability across all options at 0% and all probability on one option at 100%. We use the chosen option's ordinary probability as confidence.

    A different positive calibration temperature never changes which option wins within a question. It can change the order of confidence scores across questions. The small change in the ranking score in our version 1 adjustment was an observed result, not a rule.

    How we checked the questions and answer key

    We use version 2 of the question set. Our first copy of the questions from the book lost formatting that some grammar questions needed. Of 229 questions that pointed to underlined words, 196 had lost the underline. Another 186 questions had lost the numbers attached to words. We copied the flagged questions again and checked them against the book's page images.

    Version 2 changes the text or answer key of 375 questions. It also makes 12 more questions possible to score. A later review of the answer key removed 12 questions whose correct answers we could not confirm. We score 2,198 questions; 12 of them stay marked as uncertain. These counts come from separate checks. In total, 493 questions carry a verified label.

    We leave the removed questions out of accuracy and confidence results. The time, tokens and money we spent on them stay in the totals.

    The repair table splits the questions we changed from the questions we left unchanged. For the models we tested on both versions, accuracy on the repaired questions rose by 11 to 30 percentage points, apart from Laya and Laya multilingual, whose accuracy on them did not rise. Repeated runs can also vary. In requests of 16 questions, a repaired question can also sit next to different questions.

    The source is TYT IQ Türkçe Soru Kütüphanesi (TYT IQ Turkish Question Library), published by Paraf Yayınları (ISBN 978-625-7423-18-2; the imprint gives © 2022 and no edition number). We do not publish the copyrighted questions or page images.

    Someone with the book can rebuild a matching question set with the extraction and repair scripts in the repository's runner folder. The scripts read the book's pages into the database format our test program uses and record each repair. The answer export keeps each question's ID, topic, page and printed number, so a rebuilt set can be matched to ours question by question. The results file gives each topic's printed Turkish title next to the English label this page uses.

    What we sent to each model

    We sent each question with its lettered options. We asked chat models for the letter of their answer, with no explanation, plus a confidence score where the setup needed one. The instruction was in Turkish. We asked for a structured reply (JSON) that our program could read.

    Most chat model setups got 16 questions per request. Decision models, Luna through the Decisions API, GLiNER and the Qwen 3.8 setups got one question per request. In a separate test, Astra and Gemma also got one question per request, on 200 questions.

    For a decision model, the request holds the whole question, all the options and an instruction to find the right answer. A small connector turns that request into the format each service expects. OpenAI's Decisions API takes it under its own field names.

    We count a missing answer, a refusal or a letter outside the options as wrong. In the main runs, the Decisions API declined to answer 4 questions without giving a reason. GLM 5.3 Flash with reasoning left 128 questions without an answer: its reasoning hit the 65,536-token output limit before it answered. DeepSeek's reasoning run had the same limit and left 0 questions without an answer.

    Which settings did we use?

    For chat models on our own hardware without reasoning, we used greedy decoding: the model always takes the most likely next token. A token is a small piece of text, sometimes part of a word.

    For DeepSeek with reasoning, we used sampling, because greedy decoding made its reasoning go round in loops. Sampling picks the next token at random, weighted by the probabilities and the settings. Its settings were temperature 1.0 and top p 1.0. GLM 5.3 Flash with reasoning used its server's default sampling: temperature 1.0 and top p 0.95 (Z.ai's generation settings) and top k 20, with TensorFold's high reasoning setting.

    For Qwen's ten-answer setup, we used temperature 0.7, top p 0.8, top k 20 and a presence penalty of 1.5. The sampling temperature controls how strongly sampling favours likely tokens. Top p and top k limit the pool of candidate tokens. A presence penalty discourages tokens the model already used. These are generation settings, not confidence scores.

    For GPT, Claude and Gemini, we used the lowest reasoning setting tested here, plus extra high reasoning runs for Sonnet and Gemini Flash. Some access methods do not let us set the sampling temperature. Gemini reported no reasoning tokens at its low setting.

    Where it applied, we replaced agent instructions with a short test instruction, ignored local configuration and turned tools off. The reported runs did not browse the web or run commands. Earlier trial runs could find the source book online, so we had to turn tools off.

    Where does each confidence score come from?

    Option score. Decision models return a probability for each option. We use the score of the chosen option. GLiNER and Qwen's yes or no scoring also give option scores.

    Written estimate. GPT, Claude, Gemini, GLM and Qwen with written confidence write a confidence number next to their answer. We show that number as a percentage.

    Answer letter probability. For Gemma, DeepSeek, Qwen 3 14B and Erk, we use the probability that the model writes the chosen letter. We rescale the probabilities of the option letters so that they add up to 100%. With reasoning on, the letter comes after the reasoning. A likely next letter is not automatically a good estimate of being right.

    Agreement across ten answers. We ask Qwen the same question ten times. Confidence is the share of answers that agree with the most common answer. If eight of the ten answers say B, the score is 80%.

    These scores come from different mechanisms. We test each one against the real answers before we trust it.

    Some services round their scores. Luna through the Decisions API gave only 80 different scores across 2,194 answered questions. Celeris gave 75 across 2,198, none above 97%. Rounding makes many answers share a score, and our calculations handle those shared scores explicitly.

    How we measure uncertainty in the results

    A result from one set of questions is an estimate. A different set of questions could give a different result. We use 95% intervals to show part of that possible variation.

    For the ranking score and for accuracy among kept answers, we make 1,000 new samples by drawing from the recorded questions. A question can appear more than once in a sample. We recompute the measure on each sample. The technical name is bootstrapping. The 14 questions that share seven passages stay together in every sample.

    For small groups of answers, such as the answers at 99% confidence or higher, we use a Wilson interval. This method still gives an upper limit above zero for the error rate when a group has no observed mistakes.

    A 95% interval comes from a method built to cover the true value in about 95% of repeated studies, under its assumptions. It is not a model's confidence score. It does not mean that one answer has a 95% chance of being right.

    We compare two models on the questions where only one of them was right, with the exact McNemar test. The selected comparison table applies the Holm adjustment across 41 tests. The comparison groups apply it across all 630 pairs. The live tests report their own comparisons without this adjustment.

    The replays with a cutoff chosen on half the questions use 300 random splits of the questions into two halves. Each split keeps the questions of a shared passage in the same half. The averages describe these splits of one question set. They are not results on new question sets.

    How we measure waiting time and processing rate

    Response time is the time from sending a request until its reply finishes, measured on our computer. It includes the network and any waits before a retry. A request may hold one question or 16.

    Processing rate is the number of answered questions divided by the time during which requests were active. We show it as questions per minute. When requests overlap, we count the elapsed active time once, not the sum of the overlapping times.

    DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (reasoning on) and GLM-5.3-Flash (reasoning on) sent eight requests at a time. Gemini 3.8 Flash (high reasoning), Fastino GLiDE and GPT-6 Luna (Decisions API, beta) sent four requests at a time. These choices, along with hardware, network conditions and service limits, affect the measured rate.

    Some early runs recorded the total time but not the time of each request. For those, we take response times from later runs of the same setup. Perplexity Decider 27B v1, Jev 1.13.0, Liquid d1, Open-Jev 27B v1.1, Qwen3.8-27B (stated confidence, reasoning off) and Qwen3.8-27B (yes/no scoring) use their four later repeat runs on all 2,198 questions. GPT-6 Astra (low reasoning), Claude Opus 5.5 (low reasoning) and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (high reasoning) use their five repeat runs on the same 200 questions. Claude Sonnet 5.5 (low reasoning), Laya and Laya multilingual have no recorded response times.

    Astra's typical response time is 13.04 seconds for a request of 16 questions, from the repeated 200 question tests. A separate test with one question per request gives 6.14 seconds. For Gemma, the typical times are 3.97 seconds for 16 questions and 0.29 seconds for one question. The price and speed table names the request size beside every time.

    Some decision services report fixed output token charges even though they write no text. Their token counts follow billing rules as much as they record work. They are not comparable amounts of written reasoning.

    What these results do not establish

    The models may have seen the questions before

    The source book is available online. A model may have seen its questions or answers during training. Researchers call this contamination, and few tests measure it.19 Turning off web access during our test stops a live lookup. It cannot remove what a model saw earlier.

    We made two extra checks. We gave the 40 Turkish questions of the June 2026 TYT exam to 9 models. They got between 24 and 40 right. We tested them in October, after the exam was public, so these questions may not be new to the models either.

    In the other check, we replaced the text of the correct option with a copy of a wrong option. Astra still chose the original correct letter 31.0% of the time and the copy 28.4% of the time. The test did not establish a preference for the original letter (p = 0.19). This checks one form of answer recall. It does not rule out that the model knew the question.

    Some answer keys remain uncertain

    Wrong answer keys are common in test sets and can change model rankings.20 We reviewed 25 disputed cases. They include confident mistakes and questions where several strong models disagreed with the printed key. We confirmed 13 keys and removed 12 questions. In six of the removals, the page image did not settle the answer. In the other six, we could not establish the printed key against the other reading.

    Removing these questions raised most models' accuracy by no more than about 0.5 percentage points. It had a bigger effect on the small groups of confident mistakes. Among the wrong answers at 99% confidence or higher, the review removed six of the eight from Qwen's yes or no scoring, three of Astra's eight, two of Jev's three and two of d1's three. Later runs did not get the same targeted review.

    Decider v1.1 ran after the review, on the cleaned question set. It gave none of the 12 questions still marked uncertain a confidence of 99% or more. So its result at 99% (0 wrong of 349 kept) does not depend on the review.

    The settings and the task matter

    Reasoning, sampling, request size, numerical precision, server software and server state can change answers. On the separate 200 question tests, Astra got 197 right with one question per request. It got 195 or 196 right in its five runs with 16 questions per request. Gemma got 137 right with one question per request, and 141 in its five runs with 16, which came from an earlier server session. These tests did not establish an accuracy difference. They do not prove that the settings give matching results, either.

    This study covers one language, one book and a question set with more grammar than reading (70% of the questions are grammar). Broader claims need more datasets and better checks of what models saw in training. A confidence cutoff for a new task needs its own test.
