Method

How we tested confidence, cost and accuracy

We tested 80 models in 103 setups on a reading comprehension and grammar test in native Turkish (not translated): 2,198 questions. A setup is one model with one set of settings. We measured four things: how often each was right, whether its confidence found the answers worth keeping, whether it answered the same way again, and its cost and time.

This page covers the Turkish reading and grammar test. Page images and Hallucination explain their own methods on their pages: Page images under How the benchmark was run and Hallucination under Our method.

How we ask the models

  • Decision models: each question goes to the model with its lettered options; we use only its pick and its score for each option (Models and setups).
  • Chat models: asked in Turkish for their answer's letter and, where needed, a confidence score, in a structured reply (What we sent); the small open chat models asked for a plain answer line (Gemma 4 E4B · default reasoning · chat and LFM2.5-2.6B · default reasoning · chat) write only a last line “CEVAP: X”, with no schema and no confidence, so theirs is not measured.

What we found

No model led on every measure: accuracy, ranking answers by confidence and confidence that matches the hit rate are separate strengths. Gemma 4 31B, a chat model, scored higher than every decision model except KnowLine-31B-Gen1.5, REAX 1.3, Gemini Decision 1 · 20261111 and d1-ultra on Reading (90.9%) and Grammar (65.6%) (GLiDE 27B tied with it on Reading), but its confidence matched its hit rate less closely than the best decision models' did.

How a model gives its confidence matters. GPT-6 Luna through OpenAI's Decisions API ranked its answers better than Luna writing its confidence (ranking score 0.82 against 0.69) but got fewer right (63.6% against 68.3%), with a different access method and request size. Among decision models, its calibration gap (2.4 points) was fourth, behind d1.1 (2.0 points), Gemini Decision 1 (2.0 points) and GLiDE 27B (2.3 points).

Cheaper chat models remain alternatives. GPT-6.1 Sol scored 96.7% at an estimated cost 79% below GPT-6 Astra's (97.6%). Gemini 3.8 Flash at high reasoning scored 98.1%, and the test over all pairs did not establish a difference from Astra.

Erk-14B is Qwen3-14B with extra Turkish training by eCloud. Its calibration gap was 13.5 points against 41.0 for its base, with no accuracy difference detected (45.0% against 45.7%); the training may have changed more than language knowledge.

On Artificial Analysis' Decision Index (English tasks), Jev 1.13 leads with 58, ahead of GLiDE and Microsoft-Decision-1 (54). Our order differs. In both tests, confidence is less reliable on some question types: knowledge and reasoning there, grammar here.

The questions

The test has 656 Reading and 1,542 Grammar questions in 20 topics, so 70% are Grammar. These results cover this question bank only. We did not test whether they hold in other languages or for other tasks. Reading asks about the meaning of words, sentences and passages; Grammar asks about word endings, word types and sentence structure. We report the two separately.

Topics 1 to 6 are Reading and 7 to 20 Grammar; the Topics page gives each one's results. Each question has five options: guessing averages 20%, and always choosing E, the most common right letter, scores 22.7%. Models can favour some letters whatever the question says.8

An optional exam mix reweights the scores to the Turkish section of the first stage of Turkey's 2026 university entrance exam (TYT): 33 Reading and seven Grammar questions. It is not a score on the exam itself.

TurkishMMLU3 covers more than 10,000 Turkish questions in nine school subjects, and HELM4 sets accuracy beside calibration and efficiency. We study the decision models' own option scores, and using them to decide when another model should answer. We do not know what most models saw in training, so we cannot test whether less familiar tasks cause more mistakes.

How we checked the questions and answer key

We use version 2 of the question set. Our first copy of the book lost the underline in 196 of 229 questions that point to underlined words, and the numbers attached to words in 186 more; we copied flagged questions again and checked them against the page images. Version 2 changes the text or key of 375 questions and makes 12 more possible to score.

Wrong keys are common in test sets and can change rankings.20 We reviewed 25 disputed cases (confident mistakes, and questions where several strong models disagreed with the key), confirmed 13 keys and removed 12 questions: in six the page image did not settle the answer, and in six the printed key could not be established. We score 2,198 questions; 12 stay marked uncertain, and 493 carry a verified label.

The removal raised most models' accuracy by at most about 0.5 points but mattered more for confident mistakes. Among wrong answers at 99% or more, it removed six of eight for Qwen's yes or no scoring, three of eight for Astra, two of three for Jev 1.13 and two of three for d1. Later runs did not get this targeted review.

For models tested on both versions, accuracy on the repaired questions rose 11 to 30 points, apart from Laya and Laya Multilingual, whose accuracy on them did not rise. Removed questions stay out of accuracy and confidence, but their time, tokens and money stay in the totals.

Every setup is scored on the same frozen key, version 2. A fix needs a check against the book's key or a second reviewer, and it rescores every run under a new version; the key changelog lists every change.

Our quality control of 12 November 2026 found four scored questions (IDs 292, 3219, 3243 and 3287) whose stored question points at a passage or dialogue that our copy of the book lost: the passage is empty and there is no figure. All four are also among the English reading track's kept questions, in the English translation of the same item; none is among the Page images questions. We keep every score, denominator and the filter of the 12 questions marked uncertain as first published, so these questions count in every run as they did; this is a limit of the test, disclosed here. The quality manifest lists them with metadata only.

The source is the practice book TYT IQ Turkish Question Library (title translated) from Paraf, ISBN 978-625-7423-18-2 (© 2022, no edition number). We do not publish its copyrighted questions; with the book, the scripts in the repository's runner folder rebuild a matching set, matched by each question's ID, topic, page and number.

Models and setups

A decision model scores the options we give it, and we used only its pick and its scores. A chat model writes its answer, and some reason first. Luna through the Decisions API counts as a decision model, because the service gives a probability for each option.

A setup is one way of running a model: access, reasoning setting, questions per request and other settings. We tested 103 setups of 80 models. Each name links to its setup's page; the results file keeps each identifier, server and hardware.

Model sizes and classes

Each model's size is its maker's figure for the released model or our count of the released checkpoint's tensors (logical shapes, never quantized storage), with what it covers and the checkpoint's revision; each size says whether the model is dense or a mixture of experts (MoE: as its maker states it, or as the checkpoint we counted shows it), a mixture of experts with the parameters active per token (or "active not disclosed by maker"), and a model with per-layer embeddings its effective size where the maker states one. A model whose maker publishes no size reads "not disclosed by maker"; we never guess one from a name or from an open sibling. The details box of each Leaderboard row and each model page give the same wording.

Size by total parameters, as disclosed by the maker (for MoE models the label also shows active parameters): Tiny under 6B · Small 6B–15B · Medium over 15B up to 40B · Large over 40B. Models of undisclosed size, by standard list price per 1M input tokens: Efficient up to $0.50 · Workhorse up to $3 · Flagship over $3.

Every board with model rows (the Leaderboard and each page with the model keys) has the same Class keys: a class narrows the rows of its tables and bar charts, together with the type keys (All, Chat models, Decision models). The setups only this site ran (Erk-14B · reasoning off · chat, GLiNER2.5 Multi, GLiNER2.5-multi-Decide, Laya, Laya Multilingual and Qwen3-14B · reasoning off · chat) are in no class; they show under Class: All.

What we sent and which settings we used

Each question went with its lettered options. Chat models were asked, in Turkish, for their answer's letter and, where needed, a confidence score, in a structured reply (JSON); a connector turned the same question into each decision service's format. Most chat setups got 16 questions per request; decision models, Luna through the Decisions API, GLiNER and the Qwen 3.8 setups got one.

Gemma 4 E4B · default reasoning · chat and LFM2.5-2.6B · default reasoning · chat, small open chat models on logic's servers, were asked one question per request for a plain answer line instead: the letter on the reply's last “CEVAP: X” line, with no structured reply and no confidence, and only the request settings of logic's own runs of each model.

Claude Code and Codex runs were closed-book: no web search, no file or command tools, and an empty folder for each request. Earlier trial runs could find the source book online.

A missing answer, a refusal or a letter outside the options counts as wrong. The Decisions API declined 4 questions; GLM 5.3 Flash with reasoning left 128 unanswered when it hit the 65,536-token output limit; DeepSeek's first release with reasoning had the same limit and left 0.

How Open Jev turns option scores into probabilities

Open Jev adds a LoRA adapter, 15.5 million adjustable values (about 0.06% of the base model), and a scoring head to a Qwen model. Each option gets its own prompt, and the head starts from Qwen's preference for "Yes" over "No". Softmax turns the scores into probabilities after dividing them by 2.53, the calibration temperature (see the model card).

Another positive temperature never changes the winning option within a question, but it can reorder confidence across questions. We use the chosen option's ordinary probability, not the rescaled score Jev and Open Jev also offer. Open Jev scored 3.8 points above Qwen's yes or no scoring (p = 0.022 after adjustment), with ranking scores of 0.82 and 0.80.

Its probabilities were too low here. Its highest confidence was 64.98%, yet all 144 answers scored 50% to below 60% were right. On version 1 of the question set, a separate refit of the temperature cut the calibration gap from 22.8 to 3.0 points, while the ranking score moved from 0.8193 to 0.8191; the main results use the released model.

How we measure accuracy and confidence

Accuracy is the share of scored questions a model got right. The ranking score (AUROC) is how often a randomly chosen right answer has higher confidence than a randomly chosen wrong one, with half credit for ties: 0.50 is chance, 1.00 perfect.

Keeping only the most confident answers is called selective prediction.10 The most confident 20% takes that share of answers with the highest scores and checks how many are right; the 50% and 80% shares work the same way. They describe the kept answers only.

Calibration

Calibration means that a model's confidence matches how often its answers are right. When a calibrated model says 80%, it is right about 80% of the time. Calibration matters when someone uses the number itself, for example to decide how much to risk on an answer.

Calibration is separate from accuracy: a model can be right often and still give probabilities that are too high or too low. It is also separate from ranking: a model can usually put right answers above wrong ones and still give probabilities of the wrong size.

Calibration asks whether the percentages match the hit rate: of 100 answers scored near 80%, about 80 should be right. The calibration gap (ECE)11 sorts answers into ten confidence groups (0% to below 10%, and so on) and compares each group's average confidence with its share right. It averages these gaps, weighting larger groups more, in points. Zero means a match within the groups, not that every score is right; the chart leaves out groups with fewer than 20 answers.

What a refit is

A refit changes the confidence scores, not the model's answers. The published ranking score still uses the original scores.

The refit has three steps. Before the conversion, probabilities are clipped to between 0.000001 and 0.999999. First, each confidence p becomes log-odds: ln(p / (1 − p)). Second, the new log-odds are a stretch times the old log-odds, plus a shift. Third, the result becomes a probability again. This is Platt scaling.21 Logistic regression chooses the stretch and the shift. With a positive stretch, the refit keeps the order of the scores it is applied to. With a negative stretch, it flips that order; these models are marked "reversed".

Example: Open-Jev 27B v1.1. Its confidence usually ranked right answers above wrong ones (ranking score 0.82), but its probabilities were too low. Fitted on all questions, its stretch is 4.42 and its shift is 4.30. The table refits each answer, then averages within each band:

Better than a flat guess

Better than a flat guess compares two probability errors on those held-out questions (5 folds by passage). The Brier score is the mean squared difference between a probability and the outcome (1 if right, 0 if wrong); lower is better. The flat guess gives every answer the same probability: the model's accuracy on the questions used for fitting. Better than a flat guess = 100 × (1 − Brier after refit ÷ Brier of the flat guess), so 0% means no improvement over the flat guess and below 0% means worse. Every model uses the same five parts, and questions without a score are left out, as in the calibration gap. Hamel Husain's post on decision models also found that calibration did not improve every model.

High accuracy does not by itself force this score to be low. The score measures how much the refitted probabilities reduce Brier error compared with the model's own flat baseline. Example: GPT-6 Astra (low reasoning, 16 questions per request) is right 97.6% of the time and scores 19.0%, while Gemini 3.8 Flash (high reasoning, 16 questions per request) is right 98.1% of the time and scores 6.5%. For models with few wrong answers, the score rests on few cases (Gemini 3.8 Flash: 42 of 2,198), so it can vary more between test sets. A score near 0% means this refit gave no improvement in this test. It does not prove that no method could make the confidence useful.

Decision Score (AA formula), English reading

The English reading page shows a Decision Score computed with Artificial Analysis's formula. Artificial Analysis's formula applied to our questions. Not their Index. Per question the score is 50 × (1 + c − (p − c)²), p the probability the model gave the answer it chose and c 1 if that answer is right, else 0; a run's score is the mean over its questions. That mean equals 50 × (1 + accuracy − Brier), the Brier here being the chosen-answer Brier, the mean of (p − c)² over the chosen answers (not a multiclass Brier over every option); the export checks the two agree to 1e-9 for every run. It is computed on each decision model's first English run on the kept questions. A run with any question lacking a finite chosen-answer probability between 0 and 1 is shown as unavailable with the reason: no probability is filled in and no subset is scored. Decision models only: chat models and the I don't know runs are excluded.

What a score of 99% or more meant

At 99% or more, Jev 1.13 got 311 of 312 right (1,886 skipped), d1 273 of 274 (1,924 skipped), Decider v1 21 of 21 (2,177 skipped) and GLiDE 121 of 121 (2,077 skipped); Luna got 365 of 368 through the Decisions API (1,830 skipped) and 693 of 830 writing its confidence (1,368 skipped). Few answers leave wide intervals: the 95% interval for the error rate is 0% to 15.5% for Decider v1 and 0% to 3.1% for GLiDE.

Gemma's letter probability reached 99% on 1,782 answers, 1,450 of them right (about 81%), and fell short of it on 416, which the 99% cutoff skips. DeepSeek's first release with reasoning gave every answer at least 99% (2,198 kept, 0 skipped), and about 92% were right.

Can another confidence method make the same answers easier to judge?

We scored one fixed set of Qwen 3.8 27B answers, 1,191 right of 2,198, four ways. The ranking score was about 0.51 for written confidence, 0.78 for Qwen's option score, 0.78 for agreement across ten answers and 0.82 for Open Jev, a separately trained model. Written confidence tends to bunch at high values,12 while large models' answer probabilities can match their hit rates well.13

The most common of ten answers scored 54.3%, against 54.2% for written confidence, and only about 85% were right when all ten agreed. Several answers can repeat one mistake.

How we tell a score gap from ordinary variation

Both models answer the same questions, so we look at the questions only one got right. The exact McNemar test17 asks whether that split is too uneven for equal accuracy. Holm's method adjusts the p values for the number of tests: across 103 selected comparisons, and across all 5,253 pairs for the comparison groups (setups sharing a letter). A result from one set does not carry over to the other.

GPT-6 Astra with medium reasoning scored 0.8 points above GPT-6.1 Sol with low reasoning: of 32 questions only one got right, Astra had 25 and Sol 7. The p value was 0.0021 before adjustment; adjusted, it was 0.2 among the selected comparisons and 0.96 across all pairs, both above 0.05. A p value says how unusual so uneven a split would be at equal accuracy, not the chance that the models are equal.

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, the 1017 release, with reasoning scored 0.9 points above GPT-6.1 Terra with low reasoning (adjusted p 1). Not establishing a difference does not show equal accuracy: many language comparisons have too few questions to detect gaps this small.18

How we measure uncertainty

A result from one question set is an estimate. For the ranking score and the accuracy of kept answers, we draw 1,000 bootstrap samples of the questions with replacement, keeping the 14 questions of seven shared passages together. For small groups, such as answers at 99% or more, a Wilson interval gives an error rate above zero even with no mistakes. A 95% interval comes from a method that covers the true value in about 95% of repeated studies, under its assumptions; it is not a model's confidence.

The live tests report their comparisons without Holm's adjustment. Replays with a cutoff chosen on half the questions average 300 random splits of this one question set, not new question sets.

How we chose which answers to keep

A cheap model answers first; we keep its answer when its confidence reaches a cutoff, and a stronger model answers the rest. FrugalGPT5 and RouteLLM6 study related routing. We count the first model's cost on every question.

Three rules set the cutoff. The cutoff chosen on half the questions is chosen on one random half (a passage's questions together) and tested on the other, 300 times. The most confident quarter keeps the first model's 25% surest answers, and the fixed 99% rule every answer at 99% or more.

These are replays of recorded answers, with no new calls. The accuracy change compares the combined answers with the second model alone on the same questions. The estimated cost adds both models' calls, without the hardware of a first model we ran ourselves.

The average-cost caveat

Replay savings give every question passed to the second model the same average cost. In the live tests, the passed questions cost more, so the savings were smaller in eight of nine tests.

In the live tests, the second model answered the passed questions with new calls, on one split of 1,099 questions, and separately answered all of them alone. One split checks the replay; it cannot replace the average over 300.

The second model changes the result: in front of Claude Sonnet 5.5 at high reasoning, GLiDE answered 44.6% of the questions and Gemma 60.9%. The best pair depends on the task and the loss you accept.

Does a decision model's uncertainty (entropy over its option scores) point to the questions a stronger model gets wrong? Weakly: its most uncertain 10% of questions held 9% to 22% of those where Opus, Sonnet or Astra erred, against about 10% by chance.

What can these results tell us about understanding?

A model may answer a passage question correctly and miss a grammar rule in the same language: performance changes by task, which does not settle whether it understands. These tests measure skills models have, not how fast they learn; Mitchell and Krakauer discuss meanings of understanding,1 and Chollet separates a skill from the ability to learn new ones.2

Reading may draw on patterns shared across languages, and school grammar more on local rules; training, difficulty and format could also explain the gap. Confidence changes too: Jev 1.13's ranking score fell from 0.89 on Reading to 0.72 on Grammar, and Open Jev's from 0.89 to 0.75. An Arize evaluation reports a stronger score for Jev 1.13 on two-label English tasks, a different test.

Stronger evidence would change a question's words but not the relationship the answer depends on, then change that relationship and check the answer follows (CheckList9; McCoy, Pavlick and Linzen14). Teaching an unfamiliar rule would test learning directly. Other work looks inside models15 or tests generalisation across wording and reasoning problems.16

What changed when we repeated the same questions?

We ran 30 of the 66 one-question-per-request setups five times on all 2,198 questions, and 14 of the 37 setups with 16 per request five times on a fixed 200-question set.

On all questions, Open Jev changed no answers, Decider v1 two answers and SubQ Snap Preview nine answers; Jev 1.13 changed about 14%, d1 10%, Celeris 1 9%, Qwen's yes or no scoring 7% and Qwen with written confidence 44 answers (2%). Decider v1.2 (local) changed no answers, Decider Flash v1.2 (local) no answers, Mercury Decide about 9% and Microsoft-Decision-1 about 13%. On the 200-question set, Astra changed 1 question, Opus 20 questions and Sonnet with high reasoning 31 questions. A model can repeat the same wrong answer, so stability alone proves little.

Gemma's stored repeats, in one server session, changed 0 questions; an unsaved rerun after a restart changed some. The Gemma and DeepSeek repeats have no confidence scores.

How we measure latency and throughput

Latency is the time from sending one request to receiving its answer, measured from our computer, network and any retries within the request included; a request holds one question or 16. Request time is the time at least one request was in flight, over the requests whose answers were kept, overlaps counted once. It is not a run's elapsed time: it leaves out requests that failed and any pause before a run was resumed. Throughput is answered questions per minute of request time.

MiMo-V2.6-Flash (high reasoning), Gemma 4 E4B (default reasoning) and LFM2.5-2.6B (default reasoning) sent 32 requests at a time. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (reasoning on), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (reasoning on) and GLM-5.3-Flash (reasoning on) sent eight requests at a time. Some early runs recorded the total time but not the time of each request. For those, we take latencies from later runs of the same setup. What these results do not establish

The questions are online, so models may have seen them in training (contamination, which few tests measure19); turning off web access cannot undo that. On the 40 Turkish questions of Turkey's June 2026 university entrance exam, public before our October test, 14 models got 24 to 40 right. Of the five we added in October and checked this way, d1-ultra got 34, Jev 1.14 31, Decider v1.2 (local) 31, Decider Flash v1.2 (local) 28 and GLiDE v1.2 (local) 33. These 40 questions are a separate check, not part of the 2,198.

When we replaced the correct option's text with a copy of a wrong one, Astra chose the original letter 31.0% of the time and the copy 28.4% on the 1,660 questions whose options are text, with no established preference (p = 0.19). On the same questions, d1-ultra (12.2% and 11.9%), Jev 1.14 (8.4% and 9.3%), Decider v1.2 (local; 11.3% and 11.2%), Decider Flash v1.2 (local; 8.6% and 7.2%) and GLiDE v1.2 (local; 9.2% and 8.0%) showed no established preference either (p from 0.16 to 1). On the 328 questions whose options are single numerals, Astra, d1-ultra and Decider v1.2 chose the original letter more often, which the numerals' fixed order can explain. That checks one form of recall, not whether the model knew the question.

Reasoning, sampling, request size, precision, server software and server state can change answers. This is one language and one question source; a confidence cutoff for a new task needs its own test.
