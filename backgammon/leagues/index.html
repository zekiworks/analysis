The setup

Earlier version Plays were listed in the runner's order, which tends to put the best play early.

Each model plays every other model once per league (a single round robin). A match is played in rounds of two games with the same dice: in the second game the players swap seats and the same sequence of rolls is repeated, so both face the same luck. Games score 1 point, 2 for a gammon and 3 for a backgammon; there is no doubling cube.

  • Match length: 3 rounds, or fewer: the first player to 5 points after a full round (both games) wins if ahead. A tie gets one extra round, then counts as a draw.
  • League table: 3 points for a match win, 1 for a draw; ties broken by more games won, then fewer games lost.
  • Turn limit: a game still running after 300 turns (both sides counted) goes to the lower pip count for 1 point.
  • Clock: 30 seconds free per decision, then a 5-minute bank per game. Running out loses the game.
  • Legality: the program generates every legal play and the model chooses one of them, so a model can never make an illegal move. A roll with only one legal play is played automatically.
  • Memory: every decision is a separate request. Models are not told how earlier games went.

With seven models, each league has 21 matches and one model sits out each matchday. A match starts as soon as both of its models are free, and no model plays two matches at once.

Models

All seven answer the same kind of request: a text state and one choice question whose options are the legal plays. Two are hosted services; the other five run on our own GPUs.

TypeSafe's decision model, served through its System One API; the request format the others copy.

OpenAI's decision endpoint with gpt-6-luna. The only one that sometimes declines to choose; that happened 3 times in early leagues, on positions with 97 to 439 legal plays, and never in the current format.

The released pplx-decider-v1.1-27b checkpoint, run with its own server code. It accepts up to 8,192 tokens, so long play lists are sent in groups of 25 (see below). It replaced Perplexity's hosted Decider 27B from league #6 on.
Clef · Cloudflare, self-hosted
A 27B decision model that scores all options in one pass. Groups of up to 110 options.
Open-Jev 27B v1.1 · open reimplementation of Jev, self-hosted
A LoRA adapter and decision head on a 27B base model. It scores every option as its own sequence that repeats the whole question, with a 4,096-token limit per sequence, which is what limits the prompt's length for every model.
Cygnet · blockbrain-ai recipe, self-hosted
Google's gemma-4-12B-it, unchanged, behind a server that labels the options A, B, C… and reads the model's probability for each letter. Groups of up to 26 options.
Gemma 4 31B · our configuration of the Cygnet recipe
The same letter-reading server in front of gemma-4-31B-it, served in FP8. Not a trained decision model: a general chat model whose answer-letter probabilities are read directly. Its calibration setting was fitted for Cygnet's 12B model; that changes its reported confidence, not which play it picks.

When a position has more legal plays than a model's group size, the plays are split into equal groups, the model picks a winner in each, then chooses among the winners. Liquid's d1 played in the early leagues and was dropped after its free tier rate-limited a league to a stop.

Error rate

Earlier version Plays were listed in the runner's order, which tends to put the best play early.

Wins and losses depend heavily on the dice. A backgammon engine can instead judge every decision directly: for each move it finds the best play, and measures how much winning chance, in equity (expected points per game), the chosen play gave away. Averaged over all decisions and multiplied by 500, this is the error rate (performance rating, PR) that backgammon players use, here for checker plays only; forced moves are not counted. eXtreme Gammon's human scale: under 2.5 world champion, 2.5 to 5 world class, 5 to 7.5 expert, 7.5 to 12.5 advanced, 12.5 to 17.5 intermediate, 17.5 to 22.5 casual, 22.5 to 30 beginner, 30 or more distracted. Human ratings also count doubling cube decisions, which these games do not have, so the comparison is approximate.

We scored all 104,045 decisions the models made in every league with GNU Backgammon 1.08. Every legal play was evaluated at 0-ply, then the five best and the chosen play again at 2-ply; the loss is the 2-ply equity of the best play minus that of the chosen one. As a check, on the opening roll the plays that the rollout table calls best lose 0.0002 equity on average, other plays 0.063.

Can a team beat the best model?

Earlier version Plays were listed in the runner's order, which tends to put the best play early.

Every model reports a probability for each option. If those probabilities say when a model is right, a team could ask all seven and play the answer of whichever is most sure. To test this, we sent the same 1,000 recorded positions (2 to 25 legal plays each, drawn from leagues #8, #11, #12 and #13) to all seven models with exactly the request the original player received, and scored every legal play with GNU Backgammon at 2-ply: 7,000 answers and 9,439 evaluated plays. Any team and any rule can then be scored offline on the same positions.

Limits

Earlier version Plays were listed in the runner's order, which tends to put the best play early.

  • Small sample. Three finished leagues of 21 matches in the main format, about 340 games. Placings in the middle of the table move from league to league; the error rate is the steadier measure.
  • The engine evaluation is not the strongest available. Moves are judged at 2-ply with GNU Backgammon, not by full rollouts or eXtreme Gammon, and in cubeless money equity: the games have no doubling cube, and match score is ignored. Small differences between close plays can be wrong, but the losses that count as blunders and the gaps between models are much larger than that.
  • Models only play each other. Their absolute strength against human or engine players is unknown. On the engine's scale none of them reaches expert level; the best, GPT-6 Astra, plays like an advanced human.
  • Configuration matters. Five models run on servers set up by us, and Gemma 4 31B uses a recipe built for a different model. Hosted services can change without notice.
  • The prompt steers. The question and the strategy text say what a good move looks like, so the results measure how well models follow that guidance with the numbers in front of them, as much as how well they play.
