Setup

The question is which model, at which reasoning setting, makes better decisions in which positions. Two kinds of data answer it: league games and shared positions. A setup is one model with one set of settings: the same model at a lower and a higher reasoning effort is two setups. A setup is named by what its requests sent, checked in the request logs: (low), (medium), (high) = the effort level sent; (off) = reasoning turned off, no reasoning tokens; (on) = thinking switched on where the model offers no effort levels (its chat template's default).

Leagues

Setups play each other in leagues: each league is a single round robin. League tables compare players inside one league only, and leagues are never merged into one overall score. See Leagues.

  • League 1: decision models, plus two programs: Random (a random legal play) and Safest (the play that the fewest opponent rolls hit).
  • League 2: workhorse chat models, closed and open, each in two settings (two effort levels, or reasoning off and a reasoning setting).
  • League 3: frontier chat models played through their subscriptions, each at its lowest effort (none of them accepts reasoning off) and at its default effort.
  • Anchor: GNU Backgammon at its weak Beginner level plays in all three leagues as a fixed reference player (see the reference-player checks).

Match rules

  • A match is played in rounds of two games with the same dice: in the second game the players swap seats and the same rolls are repeated, so both face the same luck.
  • Three rounds, or fewer: the first player to 5 points after a full round wins if ahead. A tie gets one extra round, then counts as a draw. A game still running after 300 turns goes to the lower pip count for 1 point.
  • Games score 1 point, 2 for a gammon and 3 for a backgammon. There is no doubling cube.
  • League table: 3 points for a match win, 1 for a draw; ties broken by more games won, then fewer games lost.
  • Dice seeds are fixed per league and published. Each match has its own dice (see the dice-sharing test).
  • The v3 leagues play without a clock (see Time).

How a decision is asked

  • Legal plays only. The program generates every legal play and the model chooses one of them, so a model can never make an illegal move. A roll with only one legal play is played automatically and is not a decision.
  • Shuffled order. The legal plays appear in a fixed random order per position, drawn from a published seed. The order never comes from an engine's ranking.
  • Reading the answer. Decision models give a probability for each option. Open chat models give probabilities for each answer letter, read after their reasoning when it is on. Models without probabilities answer one letter.
  • Long play lists. When a position has more legal plays than a model accepts, the plays are split into equal groups. The model picks a winner in each group, then chooses among the winners. The decision page shows when this happened.
  • Invalid answers. An answer that names no option is not retried. The runner plays a random legal play and marks the decision invalid. That play is never counted as the model's choice: it is shown as invalid and left out of every error rate.
  • Retries. Network and server errors are retried. Retry and wait time is kept apart from request time.
  • Memory. Every decision is a separate request. Models are not told how earlier games went.

Game phases

Each position gets the first phase that matches:

  1. Opening: turns 1 to 6, each side's first three moves.
  2. Bear-off: any of GNU Backgammon's bear-off classes.
  3. Race: GNU Backgammon's race class.
  4. Holding: a contact position after turn 6 where one side has escaped all its back checkers and the other keeps one high anchor (2 or more checkers on the opponent's bar, 5- or 4-point, and no other checkers in the opponent's home board or on its bar point). This follows The Gammon Press's definition of a holding game.
  5. Contact: every other position.

Game-plan tags

For analysis only, each contact position after the opening gets the first tag that matches: blitz (a checker on the bar against a home board with 3 or more made points), back game (2 or more anchors in the opponent's home board), holding (the rule above), low anchor (the only anchor is on the opponent's 3-, 2- or ace-point), priming (4 or more made points in a row), or other.

The prompt

The current version is bg-v2. Every player gets it, and it did not change after the first match. It is the format of the earlier version's league #8. Every decision sends these parts in this order:

  1. Rules and strategy: the rules of backgammon, strategy principles (blots and points, distribution, exposure, blocking, hitting, anchors), the best opening plays from computer rollouts, and the match rules. Decision models get a short version; chat models get a longer one with a glossary, game plans and a worked example. Each setup's page says which it got.
  2. Score and where the match stands.
  3. Position from the mover's side: checkers on every point, bar, borne off, pip counts.
  4. Race: the pip lead and whether the two sides are still in contact.
  5. Dice, then the question: which move makes a point in front of the opponent's checkers, reduces your single pieces, or gets you closer to home.
  6. The legal plays in the shuffled order, each followed by what the position looks like after it, computed by the program: hits, points made, blots left and how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit them, points held, longest prime, pips. Chat models see each play under a letter.

There is no move history: in the earlier version, adding the last 10 moves made no measurable difference. The Every decision page shows the exact text each model was sent.

History

The prompt was built one step at a time in the earlier version's leagues; the next section shows each step and what it changed. bg-v2 keeps that format and changes how the models are asked: the plays are shuffled, an invalid answer is not retried, and every setup gets the same rules and question. The earlier version re-asked up to three times and then played the first legal play, which its order favoured.

Rules and strategy as text, not a data structure

The first leagues sent the position as structured data with a short rules summary. Models opened badly: 28% of first moves matched the known best play. With the rules, strategy principles and the opening table as prose, that rose to 77%. Opening play improved, but the middle game did not: games still ran to the turn limit, because both sides kept hitting and re-entering.

A question that names what a good move does

The question before the choices states three goals in plain words: build a point in front of the opponent's checkers, reduce your blots, bring checkers home. It deliberately leaves hitting out, because hitting on almost every move was the problem. The turn-limit share fell from 81% to 71% (this league also switched to the self-hosted Decider v1.1); hitting barely changed.

Compute the consequences in code, not in the model

Backgammon decisions turn on numbers a language model cannot work out reliably from a list of notations: which checkers are left alone, how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit them, which points are made. Earlier, each option was just its notation, like bar/19 13/11*, and the model had to imagine the resulting position. Now the program computes those numbers for every option and the model compares them. This is the change that ended the endless games: hits fell from about 60% to under 10% of moves and the average game from about 270 turns to about 50.

Opening play got worse at the same time, from about 80% to between 58% and 69% best plays. The metrics after each option compete with the opening table earlier in the prompt, and on the first move several plays look similar on these numbers.

Recent moves, for longer plans

Each decision is a separate request, so a model cannot see how the position came about. League #13 adds the last 10 moves of the game, both sides. Ten turns add about 200 tokens. More would eat into Open-Jev's 4,096-token limit, which it applies to the whole question repeated for every option.

It made no measurable difference. League #13's error rate was 39.1 (± 2.2), against 38.6 (± 1.2) for #8, #11 and #12 together, and the finishing order barely changed. Ten moves may be too few, or these models may not use them.

Error rate

Wins and losses depend heavily on the dice. A backgammon engine can instead judge every decision directly. GNU Backgammon 1.08 evaluates every legal play at 2-ply, in cubeless money equity (expected points per game, match score ignored). The loss of a decision is the equity of the best play minus the equity of the play made. A play that ends the game scores its points exactly.

Error rate
500 × mean 2-ply equity loss per decision. Lower is better. This is the checker-play performance rating (PR) that backgammon players use.
Decision
A roll with 2 or more legal plays. Forced moves count in no rate.
Invalid answers
Left out of the error rate and shown separately, with their count and share.
Engine's best play
Share of decisions where the play made lost less than 0.001 equity.
Blunder rate
Share of decisions where the play made lost more than 0.08 equity.
Tail error
The 0.95 quantile of the loss per decision, and the mean loss of the worst 0.05 of decisions.
Near-tie
The two best plays are within 0.02 equity. Close plays are not rolled out; the gap groups on the Phases page show how much of a setup's error comes from them.

Was it the right risk? Definitions

The core question: when a model takes a risk, is it the right risk? And when it plays safe, was safe right? The thresholds below (0.001, 0.02, the stake bands 0.02 and 0.08) were fixed on 2026-10-23, before any of these numbers was computed.

Hitting rolls
For each legal play, how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit, read from the numbers the model was shown next to that play.
Minimum risk
The fewest hitting rolls any legal play leaves in that position.
Best safe play
Among the plays that leave the minimum risk, the one the engine rates best.
Stake
The equity difference between the engine's best play and the best safe play: how much the risk call is worth in that position.
Position class
No trade-off: every legal play leaves the same number of hitting rolls; counted and left out of every risk metric. Safety pays: stake below 0.001. Risk pays: stake 0.001 or more.
Took a risk
The chosen play leaves more hitting rolls than the minimum. Otherwise the model played safe.
Risk call
Right risk: took a risk, and the chosen play is at least 0.001 better than the best safe play. Wrong risk: took a risk in a Risk pays position, but not one at least 0.001 better than the best safe play. Needless risk: took a risk in a Safety pays position. Missed risk: played safe in a Risk pays position. Right safety: played safe in a Safety pays position. Every decision with a trade-off gets exactly one risk call.

Metrics per setup (the Risk tab, in this order)

  1. Risk judgement: the average of risks found and right safety, × 100. It does not depend on how many positions of each class a setup met. With both classes present, the Safest program scores exactly 50, the "always safe" line. If a setup has no Risk pays or no Safety pays decisions, it shows "N/A: no <class> decisions"; an empty class is never read as 0 or 100. 95% interval: bootstrap by game.
  2. Risks found: right risks ÷ Risk pays decisions.
  3. Right safety: right safety ÷ Safety pays decisions.
  4. Good risks: right risks ÷ all risks taken.
  5. Missed risks: missed risks ÷ Risk pays decisions (too cautious).
  6. Needless risks: needless risks ÷ Safety pays decisions (too bold).
  7. Wrong risks: wrong risks ÷ Risk pays decisions.
  8. Error rate by risk-call category: each part is 500 × the equity lost in that category ÷ all scored decisions of the setup, the same denominator for all six parts (needless risks, missed risks, wrong risks, right risks, right safety, No trade-off), so they add up exactly to the error rate. A right risk or right safety can still lose equity: the chosen play need not be the engine's best. It says where losses happened, not what caused them.
  9. Decisions with a trade-off, and the counts per class.

Every share shows its count and denominator. Every metric is computed per league; league comparisons are not paired. Setups met different positions, and risk judgement is not adjusted for how hard they were; paired comparisons wait for the shared positions.

Breakdowns

  • By stake (Risk pays only): small (below 0.02), medium (0.02 to 0.08), big (above 0.08); risks found, missed risks and wrong risks per band, and "big risks found" on its own.
  • Needless risks have no stake band (their stake is below the margin by definition); they are banded by the equity the chosen play lost, with the same limits, and "big needless risks" are those that lost more than 0.08.
  • Risk size: extra hitting rolls over the minimum, averaged for right risks and for wrong and needless risks. An average over fewer than 200 decisions shows "Too few decisions".
  • By phase: risk judgement per phase, on the Phases page and through the home page's Phase filter.
  • Confidence on the call (setups with probabilities only): among the risks with a probability, the ranking score (AUROC) of confidence for "right risk", and the average confidence on missed risks. Decisions whose plays were split into groups have no single probability and are left out; each row shows how many. The confidence is the probability a model gave its chosen play, not the probability that the risk beats the best safe play, so no calibration gap is reported for risk calls. The calibration gap is reported only for the event the confidence stands for, the chosen play being the engine's best (Confidence tab).

Ability and caution

Results published: ability, caution, aim and the cost-derived SDT reference are on the home page's Risk tab (table and chart).

In plain words: ability is how well a setup tells Risk pays positions from Safety pays positions; caution is how much evidence it needs before it takes a risk. A setup can be able and cautious, able and bold, and so on; the two are read separately. Both use the risk classes and risk calls above, at each margin, for every setup and reference row with decisions.

  • Hit rate = risks taken in Risk pays positions (right + wrong risks) ÷ Risk pays decisions. False-alarm rate = needless risks ÷ Safety pays decisions.
  • Both rates are corrected before the formulas: 0.5 is added to the count and 1.0 to the total (log-linear correction), so a rate of 0 or 1 still has a finite z. The raw counts and totals are shown next to every value.
  • Ability d′ = z(hit rate) − z(false-alarm rate), z the inverse of the standard normal distribution. Caution c = −(z(hit rate) + z(false-alarm rate)) ÷ 2: positive is cautious, negative is bold. 95% intervals resample blocks of games (the two games of a round, seats swapped, same dice), 2000 times; a resample without one of the two classes is left out.
  • Aim = right risks ÷ (right + wrong risks): needless risks are not in it, so it is not the same as Good risks. With no right or wrong risk it shows N/A and the count 0 / 0.
  • Missing class: a setup without Risk pays or without Safety pays decisions shows "N/A: no <class> decisions" for ability, caution and the reference. The correction is never used to stand in for an absent class.
  • Cost-derived SDT reference c* = ln β ÷ d′, with β = (Safety pays ÷ Risk pays decisions) × (mean equity lost per needless risk ÷ mean equity lost per missed risk), from the setup's own decisions at that margin. It is the caution at which an equal-variance signal-detection observer with these costs would lose least; it is not the best caution for actual play and not a proven optimum. The costs are conditional on the setup's own policy (which positions it took risks in), and a correctly classified call can still lose equity. Rules, in order: N/A when a class is absent; N/A with no needless or no missed risk to price; N/A when a mean cost is 0 or β is not a positive finite number; N/A when d′ ≤ 0; "unstable: d′ near zero" (value shown, no mark) when the lower end of the d′ interval is 0 or below; "unstable" (value shown, no mark) when fewer than 200 needless or missed risks are behind a mean cost. Only the remaining values get the short reference mark on the chart.
  • By reasoning setting (Leagues 2 and 3): ability and caution of the same model at its lower and higher setting, the change, and the time and output tokens per decision. UNPAIRED: the two settings met different positions.
  • Limits: a simple model of the risk call (equal-variance signal detection); setups met different positions; one kind of risk only (being hit). These rules were fixed on 2026-10-24 before any value was computed.

Reference rows

Shown, never ranked, never champions. Random plays a random legal play; GNUbg weak is the x86-64 build: its noise is shifted, so it overplays gammons (see The anchor).

Safest is not the same thing in every league. In League 1, Safest is the program that actually played the matches: among the plays that leave the fewest hitting rolls it takes the one with the fewest blots, then the largest pip lead, then the play shown first; it never asks the engine. It played a fewest-hitting-rolls play in 2,058 of its 2,058 scored decisions and the engine's best safe play in 1,395 of them. Its right-safety part of the error rate, 8.45 PR, is the equity lost on its 749 right-safety decisions × 500 ÷ all 2,058 of its scored decisions: a share of its error rate, not the average loss of a right-safety decision, which is 23.22 PR. In League 2 and League 3, Safest (computed) is a computed reference: the engine's best safe play on every scored decision of that league. Its numbers are not directly comparable with League 1's played program.

Random also played League 1 only; in the other leagues it is computed as the expectation over all legal plays, so its counts can have decimals. A cell with no decisions shows its count, 0, and no average.

Statistics and verdicts

  • Intervals are 0.95 bootstrap intervals with 2000 resamples and a fixed seed (20261021).
  • League decisions resample whole games, because moves within a game are not independent.
  • Shared positions resample positions.
  • Comparisons of two setups on the same positions are always paired: the interval is of the per-position difference in loss.
  • League comparisons are not paired. League games give each setup different positions. Between league setups we write only "no clear difference in league play" or "lower/higher error rate in league play", never a verdict label.
  • Minimum sample: an average (error rate, mean equity lost, a calibration metric) over fewer than 200 decisions shows "Too few decisions" instead of a value. Raw counts are always shown, and a share is always shown with its count and denominator. Exception: the 50-position error rates on New models: first look are shown with their intervals.

Re-read, refit, bar and distributions (offline, from recorded decisions)

Results published: the re-read, calibration and distributions on the Confidence tab, the minimum bar on the Cost tab.

Four analyses of the decisions already recorded; no model was asked again. Every rule below (thresholds, estimators, exclusions, degenerate cases, splits and the hypothesis) was fixed and committed on 2026-10-24, before any of these numbers was computed. Splits, folds and resamples use one map of blocks per league for every setup: a block is the two games of a round, the second with seats swapped and the same dice (checked on the data: same rolls, and no two matches share a dice seed).

Which probabilities are complete

A decision enters these analyses only if its option probabilities are verifiably complete. Each one is checked against the raw response: the response must reproduce the stored probabilities, and every legal play must have its own probability in it: every play for a decision model, every option letter among the returned top log-probabilities for a letter readout. A letter missing there was filled with 0 and the rest renormalised by the runner; such a decision is sparse, whatever the length or sum of its vector. Decisions split into groups, choice-only answers, invalid answers, decisions without a raw response and decisions whose stored vector is not reproduced are counted separately and left out (Confidence tab, per setup).

Re-read risk call (re-read from the model's probabilities, not played)

  • P(risk) = the probability mass on the plays that leave more hitting rolls than the minimum. If P(risk) ≥ t the re-read takes the most probable risky play, otherwise the most probable safe play; equal probabilities go to the play shown first. A decision without a trade-off keeps the play made.
  • t is chosen from 0.0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7, 0.75, 0.8, 0.85, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0, always safe ("always safe" never takes a risk, 0 always does) to maximise risk judgement on half of the league's blocks; ties go to the value nearest 0.5, then the smaller. It is scored on the other half, against the play made on exactly the same decisions: risk judgement on the decisions with a trade-off, error rate on all of them.
  • 300 splits; reported: the mean over splits, and the 5th to 95th percentile of the change over the splits as stability (the splits are not independent experiments). 95% interval: the whole procedure repeated on 200 resamples of blocks (30 splits each). Setups with fewer than 200 complete decisions show "Too few decisions".
  • Recorded positions scored again, not new games: it says nothing about win rates.

Hypothesis, recorded before computing: gains will be largest for setups with high ability whose caution is well above the cost-derived SDT reference. It is a hypothesis, not a claim that the reference is optimal. Predictor, from the training half only: d′ × (c − c*); undefined where c* is N/A or d′ is below 0.2; a setup needs it in 150 splits. Test: Spearman rank correlation with the held-out change in risk judgement across setups, leagues pooled; supported only if it is positive and its 95% interval is above 0. Result: ρ = −0.15 (95% interval −0.46 to +0.03, 200 resamples) over 24 setups: not supported.

Calibration as shipped and after a refit

Two events, never mixed: the chosen play (its probability against "the chosen play is the engine's best") and the risk class (P(risk) against "the position is Risk pays", decisions with a trade-off). The refit is Platt scaling (a logistic curve on the log-odds), cross-fitted over 5 folds of blocks: no decision is refitted by a curve that saw its own games. A fold whose other folds hold one outcome only, or whose fit does not converge, is left out of both before and after. Brier score and a reliability diagram in 10 fixed bins, before and after, on the same decisions. The refit is not used to choose anything else.

Minimum bar, then cheapest

Bar: risk judgement at least 60 and error rate at most 35 PR (equity lost, not a share of mistakes). In each split, the model setups that clear the bar on the training half qualify, and the cheapest of them with a price on every training decision is chosen; it is then checked on the other half. Shown: how often each setup qualified, was chosen and cleared the bar on the held-out half; splits where none qualified, or only unpriced setups did. An unpriced setup is never called cheapest; programs are left out (Cost tab).

Confidence on right and wrong decisions

The chosen play's probability (never P(risk)), complete decisions only, in 10 fixed bins: decisions that were the engine's best play against the rest, and right risks against wrong and needless risks (0.001 margin).

Stage A: results published (Confidence tab, model pages, Every decision filter). Stage B: five teams, results published. Gemma 4 31B (on) stopped, not reported. (home page, Stage B: five teams).

A decision is a close choice when the second most likely play has at least half the probability of the first (internally: split). Probabilities over plays, not sampled reasoning. The rules below were fixed and committed on 2026-10-24 before any close-choice number was computed. Inspired by "Can LLMs Anticipate Their Cruxes?". They describe the probabilities a setup gave; they say nothing about how it reasoned or why it chose.

Stage A: from recorded decisions

  • Which decisions. Only decisions whose record gives a probability for every legal play (decided by the record's content, not by model type; the check is described under Which probabilities are complete). Left out and counted per setup: invalid answers, decisions asked in groups of options, incomplete distributions and records that cannot be verified. Setups that name one play only, and programs, show N/A. No model was asked again.
  • The rule. Take the two most probable plays, with probabilities p1 and p2. A decision is a close choice when p2 is at least half of p1. Both are kept for every decision, with p1 + p2, so two strong candidates can be told apart from probability spread over many options. Equal probabilities are ordered by the order the plays were shown to the model, never by the engine; how many decisions have such a tie in the top two is reported.
  • What differs between the two plays. From the option text the model was shown: the opponent's hitting rolls, the newly made points, both pip counts and the longest prime. Every feature that differs is recorded; when several differ, none is picked as the cause. Both plays' GNUbg 2-ply values, the gap between them and how far each is behind the engine's best play (two plays can be close to each other and both lose a lot).
  • Risk. A close choice is between a safe play and a riskier one when one of the two plays leaves the fewest hitting rolls of all legal plays and the other leaves more. By stake band, in Risk pays positions only: close choices among all eligible decisions in the band. A big stake means playing safe costs more by the engine's value; it is not how hard the decision was. Both margins (0.001, 0.02); the rule for a close choice is the same at both.
  • Missed risks. In Risk pays positions whose two most probable plays are a safe one and a riskier one: how often the play made was a missed risk, among close choices and among the other decisions. The other decisions are weighted to the close choices' mix of phase, number of options and the engine's gap between its two best plays (only groups holding both count); the crude rates are shown too. 95% interval: 2000 resamples of matches (a match's decisions stay together). Empty groups show N/A. An association, not a cause.
  • Reading it. A high or low share of close choices is neither good nor bad; setups are never sorted or ranked by it. Shares come with count and denominator; under 200 decisions they are marked.

Stage B: five teams: does a setup know where it hesitates?

  • Positions. 100 positions from the new half of the shared standard set, by risk class at the 0.001 margin: 40 Risk pays, 40 Safety pays and 20 No trade-off, spread over the phases. Within each risk class and phase, the positions are picked by a seeded hash rule (seed text close-choices-b-v2-2026-10-24:); the frozen file's sha256 is 88bc3904…48410c. Race and bear-off positions have no contact, so they are all No trade-off; the Risk pays and Safety pays places of those phases go to opening, contact and holding. Every call for a position uses the same option order.
  • Calls. Each setup answers every position 5 times, and once more in a separate call that names the features it expects to differ between the plays it might choose. Every setup keeps its league settings exactly; a setup that gives the same answer every time is reported as deterministic on these positions, and settings are never changed to create variation. The separate call gets everything the play call gets (rules and strategy text, position, options in the same order) except the answer format: its question, a schema for the features and a neutral system prompt instead of the letter instruction. Before the run, one call per provider route on a position outside every shared set checks only that the format is accepted.
  • One run. Each setup is run once: at most 2 retries of network or server errors per call, no rerun, and no invalid answer is asked again.
  • Scoring. Observed set: the features of stage A that differ between any two of the plays chosen in the 5 answers. Predicted set: the features named in the separate call. Primary score: exact agreement of predicted and observed feature sets. Secondary score: partial overlap |P and O| / |P or O|; 1 when both empty, 0 when exactly one empty. A position is scored only when the prediction and all 5 answers are valid; left-out positions are reported with their reasons.
  • Baseline. The predictions shuffled across positions within the same setup and phase, 1000 seeded shuffles, scored the same way.
  • Limits. 5 repeats are not a measure of a model's whole behaviour. Stage B results are shown separately from stage A, per team against its own baseline, never as a ranking.

Time

  • Request time runs from sending a request to receiving the answer. Time spent on retries and on waiting after provider errors is kept separate and is not part of it.
  • Several requests can be in flight at once, so on our own hardware the time depends on the load.
  • Time per decision is shown as the median and the 90th percentile.
  • Time budget: the share of decisions slower than 30 seconds.
  • The v3 leagues had no clock, so no game could be lost on time. The risk of going over a time budget in play was therefore not measured; the share above only shows how often a setup needed more time.

Limits

  • One kind of risk. The risk calls measure one kind of risk, being hit. Gammon risk and priming are not in it.
  • Different positions. Setups played different positions; league comparisons are not paired, and risk judgement is not adjusted for how hard the positions were.
  • Checker plays only. The games have no doubling cube, so the error rate leaves out cube decisions. The human scale is a rough guide.
  • The engine evaluation is not the strongest available. Plays are judged at 2-ply with GNU Backgammon, not by rollouts, in cubeless money equity with match score ignored. Small differences between close plays can be wrong. GNU Backgammon learned standard play, so its scores for early Nackgammon positions are likely less accurate.
  • The prompt steers. The strategy text and the question say what a good move looks like. The results measure how well models follow that guidance with the numbers in front of them, as much as how well they play.
  • Strategy text is in training data. Rules, opening tables and strategy articles are online. Backgammon is not an unfamiliar task.
  • Setups get different inputs. Rules text length and option groups differ between setups; rankings mark rows that got different inputs. The shared positions avoid group splits.
  • Providers vary. A hosted model can change without notice, and providers differ in quantization. We log them; we cannot control them.
  • Models only play each other and the anchor. Their strength against human players is not measured.

The anchor

The anchor, GNU Backgammon 1.08 at its Beginner level (0-ply, deterministic noise 0.06), ran on one x86-64 build only, and on x86-64 it is not the Beginner that GNU Backgammon intends. Its deterministic-noise code sums the hash bytes as char, which is signed on x86-64, but subtracts the mean of unsigned bytes, so every noise draw is shifted by about −7 standard deviations. The anchor understates its own winning chances and plays for gammons.

  • Another build plays differently. On the 1,000 v1 positions, the x86-64 and ARM builds of the same package choose different plays in 659; with the noise off, in 6, all exact ties. Error rate 21.8 on x86-64 against 16.8 on ARM.
  • Gammons in self-play. 72% of games end in a gammon or backgammon on x86-64, 40% on ARM and about 30% without noise.

So v3's anchor is "GNUbg 1.08 Beginner, x86-64 build", and its results are reproducible on x86-64 only: a replay there gives the recorded plays. Later releases of the benchmark use a corrected build.

Method experiments

Two experiments with GNU Backgammon players only, run to choose how the leagues work. They are method findings, not results about any model.

Sharing dice across matches

Method finding, not a model result

Each match has its own dice. We tested whether giving round r of every match the same roll schedule would make a league table less a matter of luck. The test used 8 GNU Backgammon players of known strength and no model, and replayed a round robin 500 times under each scheme. The players' order was checked in a separate long run of 11,200 matches: in all 28 pairs the stronger player scored more league points per match.

  • Shared schedules made the table noisier. League points scattered more: standard deviation ×1.10 (95% interval 1.06 to 1.14); variance ×1.20 (1.11 to 1.29).
  • The order got slightly worse. Rank correlation with the true order fell from 0.890 to 0.864 (difference −0.026, 95% interval −0.038 to −0.014). The stronger of two neighbours finished above the weaker in about 72% of replays under both schemes.
  • Why. Mirrored games already cancel most of the dice luck within a match. Sharing schedules across matches cancels nothing more, and it puts all of a player's matches on the same dice, so their luck adds up: two matches of the same player correlated +0.042 with shared schedules against +0.001 without.

So the leagues keep separate dice per match. The test players are not language models, whose play varies more from match to match.

Checks of the reference player

Method finding, not a model result

The anchor is GNU Backgammon 1.08 at its Beginner level (0-ply, deterministic noise). We checked whether it is the same player on every machine.

  • It is reproducible on x86-64. Replaying all 4,387 recorded anchor decisions on the x86-64 build gives the recorded play in 4,387; on an ARM build of the same package, in 31.0%.
  • Another build plays differently. On the 1,000 v1 positions, the x86-64 and ARM builds choose different plays in 659. With the noise off, they differ in 6, all exact ties, so the engine's own scoring does not depend on the build. Error rate 21.8 on x86-64 against 16.8 on ARM.
  • Why. The noise code sums hash bytes as char, which is signed on x86-64, but subtracts the mean of unsigned bytes. On x86-64 every noise draw is shifted by about −6.9 standard deviations: the anchor understates its own winning chances and plays for gammons.
  • Gammons in self-play (300 games each): 71.7% of games end in a gammon or backgammon on x86-64, 40.3% on ARM, 30.0% without noise.

So the v3 anchor is "GNU Backgammon 1.08 Beginner, x86-64 build". It is a fixed reference, not GNU Backgammon's Beginner as published elsewhere. See also The anchor.
