Decision models play backgammon

Seven decision models play full backgammon leagues against each other. A decision model does not write moves: it is given a description of the position and the complete list of legal plays, and it picks one. We changed what that description contains several times and watched how the games changed.

Updated 9 October 2026 · 12 leagues run, 4 finished with the current prompt · every decision scored by GNU Backgammon

Finding

Describing, for every legal play, what the position looks like after it (blots left, how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit one of them, points made, pips) changed the games more than anything else. GNU Backgammon's error rate fell from about 130 to about 38: each move gave away less than a third as much equity as before. About four in five games used to run to the 300-turn limit; after the change none did.

Even so, every model plays far below a casual human: the best, Gemma 4 31B, has an error rate of 25, where casual players are usually above 10 and world-class players below 3.5. Gemma 4 31B won three of the four finished leagues and Jev 1.13.0 one; Open-Jev 27B finished last in all four.

An All-star team that asks all seven and plays the most confident answer does not beat Gemma 4 31B alone: confidence shows how hard a position is, not which model is right.

The setup

Each model plays every other model once per league (a single round robin). A match is played in rounds of two games with the same dice: in the second game the players swap seats and the same sequence of rolls is repeated, so both face the same luck. Games score 1 point, 2 for a gammon and 3 for a backgammon; there is no doubling cube.

  • Match length: 3 rounds, or fewer: the first player to 5 points after a full round (both games) wins if ahead. A tie gets one extra round, then counts as a draw.
  • League table: 3 points for a match win, 1 for a draw; ties broken by more games won, then fewer games lost.
  • Turn limit: a game still running after 300 turns (both sides counted) goes to the lower pip count for 1 point.
  • Clock: 30 seconds free per decision, then a 5-minute bank per game. Running out loses the game.
  • Legality: the program generates every legal play and the model chooses one of them, so a model can never make an illegal move. A roll with only one legal play is played automatically.
  • Memory: every decision is a separate request. Models are not told how earlier games went.

With seven models, each league has 21 matches and one model sits out each matchday. A match starts as soon as both of its models are free, and no model plays two matches at once.

Models

All seven answer the same kind of request: a text state and one choice question whose options are the legal plays. Two are hosted services; the other five run on our own GPUs.

Jev 1.13.0 · TypeSafe, hosted
TypeSafe's decision model, served through its System One API; the request format the others copy.
GPT-6 Luna · OpenAI Decisions API, hosted
OpenAI's decision endpoint with gpt-6-luna. The only one that sometimes declines to choose; that happened 3 times in early leagues, on positions with 97 to 439 legal plays, and never in the current format.
Decider v1.1 27B · Perplexity, self-hosted
The released pplx-decider-v1.1-27b checkpoint, run with its own server code. It accepts up to 8,192 tokens, so long play lists are sent in groups of 25 (see below). It replaced Perplexity's hosted Decider 27B from league #6 on.
Clef · Cloudflare, self-hosted
A 27B decision model that scores all options in one pass. Groups of up to 110 options.
Open-Jev 27B v1.1 · open reimplementation of Jev, self-hosted
A LoRA adapter and decision head on a 27B base model. It scores every option as its own sequence that repeats the whole question, with a 4,096-token limit per sequence, which is what limits the prompt's length for every model.
Cygnet · blockbrain-ai recipe, self-hosted
Google's gemma-4-12B-it, unchanged, behind a server that labels the options A, B, C… and reads the model's probability for each letter. Groups of up to 26 options.
Gemma 4 31B · our configuration of the Cygnet recipe
The same letter-reading server in front of gemma-4-31B-it, served in FP8. Not a trained decision model: a general chat model whose answer-letter probabilities are read directly. Its calibration setting was fitted for Cygnet's 12B model; that changes its reported confidence, not which play it picks.

When a position has more legal plays than a model's group size, the plays are split into equal groups, the model picks a winner in each, then chooses among the winners. Liquid's d1 played in the early leagues and was dropped after its free tier rate-limited a league to a stop.

The prompt

Every decision sends the same parts in the same order:

  1. Rules and strategy (about 6,400 characters): the rules of backgammon, strategy principles (blots and points, distribution, exposure, blocking, hitting, anchors), the best opening plays from computer rollouts, and the match rules above. A longer version with a glossary, game plans and a worked example goes to chat models; decision models get this short one because of Open-Jev's 4,096-token limit.
  2. Score and time: match score, round and game, turn number, both clocks.
  3. Position from the mover's side: checkers on every point, bar, borne off, pip counts.
  4. Race: the pip lead and whether the two sides are still in contact.
  5. The last 10 moves of this game, both sides, in the mover's numbering (league #13 on).
  6. Dice, then the question: Which of the following moves makes a point in front of the opponent's checkers, or reduces your single pieces, or gets you closer to your home?
  7. The legal plays, each followed by what the position looks like after it, computed by the program.

Rules and strategy as text, not a data structure

The first leagues sent the position as structured data with a short rules summary. Models opened badly: 28% of first moves matched the known best play. With the rules, strategy principles and the opening table as prose, that rose to 77%. Opening play improved, but the middle game did not: games still ran to the turn limit, because both sides kept hitting and re-entering.

A question that names what a good move does

The question before the choices states three goals in plain words: build a point in front of the opponent's checkers, reduce your blots, bring checkers home. It deliberately leaves hitting out, because hitting on almost every move was the problem. The turn-limit share fell from 81% to 71% (this league also switched to the self-hosted Decider v1.1); hitting barely changed.

Compute the consequences in code, not in the model

Backgammon decisions turn on numbers a language model cannot work out reliably from a list of notations: which checkers are left alone, how many of the opponent's 36 rolls hit them, which points are made. Earlier, each option was just its notation, like bar/19 13/11*, and the model had to imagine the resulting position. Now the program computes those numbers for every option and the model compares them. This is the change that ended the endless games: hits fell from about 60% to under 10% of moves and the average game from about 270 turns to about 50.

Opening play got worse at the same time, from about 80% to between 58% and 69% best plays. The metrics after each option compete with the opening table earlier in the prompt, and on the first move several plays look similar on these numbers.

Recent moves, for longer plans

Each decision is a separate request, so a model cannot see how the position came about. League #13 adds the last 10 moves of the game, both sides. Ten turns add about 200 tokens. More would eat into Open-Jev's 4,096-token limit, which it applies to the whole question repeated for every option.

It made no measurable difference. League #13's error rate was 39.1 (± 2.2), against 38.6 (± 1.2) for #8, #11 and #12 together, and the finishing order barely changed. Ten moves may be too few, or these models may not use them.

Error rate

Wins and losses depend heavily on the dice. A backgammon engine can instead judge every decision directly: for each move it finds the best play, and measures how much winning chance, in equity (expected points per game), the chosen play gave away. Averaged over all decisions and multiplied by 500, this is the error rate (performance rating, PR) that backgammon players use. As a rough guide for humans: below 3.5 is world class, 5 to 8 advanced, above 10 casual.

We scored all 104,045 decisions the models made in every league with GNU Backgammon 1.08. Every legal play was evaluated at 0-ply, then the five best and the chosen play again at 2-ply; the loss is the 2-ply equity of the best play minus that of the chosen one. As a check, on the opening roll the plays that the rollout table calls best lose 0.0002 equity on average, other plays 0.063.

What the numbers say

  • Gemma 4 31B is the strongest player here, and it is not a trained decision model: a general chat model whose answer-letter probabilities are read. It lost 3 of 18 matches and won the most gammons.
  • Safe is not the same as good. GPT-6 Luna picks the safest play most often and leaves the fewest shots, yet finished 5th in all three leagues. Winning also takes making points and building a prime, which costs some risk; Gemma and Clef take more risk and win more gammons.
  • Open-Jev 27B collapsed in this format. It finished 2nd in league #3, where options were just notations, and last in every league since, with the most shots left and the highest error rate. We have not found out why; the numbers it is shown are the same as everyone else's.
  • Speed does not decide it. The three fastest models are spread across the table, and no model came close to its clock.

Can a team beat the best model?

Every model reports a probability for each option. If those probabilities say when a model is right, a team could ask all seven and play the answer of whichever is most sure. To test this, we sent the same 1,000 recorded positions (2 to 25 legal plays each, drawn from leagues #8, #11, #12 and #13) to all seven models with exactly the request the original player received, and scored every legal play with GNU Backgammon at 2-ply: 7,000 answers and 9,439 evaluated plays. Any team and any rule can then be scored offline on the same positions.

Confidence says how hard a position is, not who is right

Within each model, confidence does carry information: across its league decisions, a model is right more often when it is more confident (AUROC 0.66 to 0.74, where 0.5 is chance). But the models use very different scales:

  • The best player costs most and is slowest. Gemini 3.8 Flash makes the fewest errors, at about ten times the price of GPT-6 Luna and with one decision in ten taking 8 seconds or more.
  • The hosted decision services are fastest and cheapest per decision, at about 0.2 s and well under a dollar per 1,000 decisions, but they play worse than Gemma 4 31B.

Limits

  • Small sample. Three finished leagues of 21 matches in the main format, about 340 games. Placings in the middle of the table move from league to league; the error rate is the steadier measure.
  • The engine evaluation is not the strongest available. Moves are judged at 2-ply with GNU Backgammon, not by full rollouts or eXtreme Gammon, and in cubeless money equity: the games have no doubling cube, and match score is ignored. Small differences between close plays can be wrong, but the losses that count as blunders and the gaps between models are much larger than that.
  • Models only play each other. Their absolute strength against human or engine players is unknown. The engine shows that all of them play far below strong human level.
  • Configuration matters. Five models run on servers set up by us, and Gemma 4 31B uses a recipe built for a different model. Hosted services can change without notice.
  • The prompt steers. The question and the strategy text say what a good move looks like, so the results measure how well models follow that guidance with the numbers in front of them, as much as how well they play.

The leagues are run by jevgammon, our league runner: it generates the legal plays, computes the numbers after each play, sends the requests and records every move, request and answer.
